## Abstract

A compact convex body $K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ is a first-order structure in two natural languages: $L_{\mathrm{aff}}$, with a ternary betweenness relation and convex-combination operations, and the sparser $L_B$, with betweenness alone. Isomorphism means affine equivalence in the first and, by a theorem of Shiffman, projective equivalence in the second. We ask when elementary equivalence already determines the body. Main theorem: two compact convex bodies of any dimension, with no regularity hypotheses, are elementarily equivalent in $L_{\mathrm{aff}}$ if and only if they are affinely equivalent. The proof rests on a definable compact family of gauges: simplices stationary for barycentric coordinates, with volume bounded below via the anticomplementary simplex. For $L_B$ we develop an interior von Staudt calculus, all quantifiers ranging over the body, making harmonic conjugacy, rational cross-ratio comparisons, and cross-ratio equality first-order; only the harmonic primitive is needed, with a single interior perspectivity effecting the change of scale. Consequences: the closed unit ball is separated from $\{\sum x_i^4\le 1\}$ in $L_B$ for every $n\ge 2$; and in the plane, projective categoricity holds outright for convex polygons and for bodies with real-analytic, positively curved, non-conic boundary, the latter via a new finite projective invariant, the conic-cluster set: the points whose every boundary arc contains six co-conic extreme points. A general reduction isolates what remains of the projective conjecture: recovery of boundary coordinates in dimension at least three, and a definable compact gauge, obstructed exactly by non-compact projective symmetry, as on the quadric. We also classify the natural noncompact examples.

## 1.Introduction

Following Stone’s barycentric calculus[14](see also[8,10]), a convex setK⊆ℝnK\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}may be
viewed as an algebra with one binary operation for eachλ∈[0,1]\lambda\in[0,1],Cλ​(p,q)=(1−λ)​p+λ​q.C_{\lambda}(p,q)=(1-\lambda)p+\lambda q.

Alternatively, discarding the operations and keeping only their common trace,KKmay be viewed as a relational structure with the single ternarybetweennessrelationB​(a,x,b)⇔x∈[a,b].B(a,x,b)\iff x\in[a,b].

Each choice determines a first-order language, and hence a notion of
elementary equivalence for convex sets. The question addressed in this paper
is:

Given two convex bodies inℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}, when are they elementarily
equivalent, relative to a specified geometric language?

The two languages calibrate differently. Isomorphism in the barycentric
language is exactly affine equivalence (Theorem3.1), while
isomorphism in the betweenness language is exactly projective equivalence
(Theorem3.4); the latter rests on the fundamental
theorem of projective geometry for maps defined on domains, in the form given
by Shiffman[12], and we prove it here in full generality: for
open or closed convex sets inℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}(n≥2n\geq 2), bounded or not. Thus a
betweenness structure cannot see parallelism, equal spacing, or midpoints;
what it can see is incidence, and — as we show — the full harmonic (hence
projective) calculus, carried out entirely in the interior of the body
(Section9).

In the affine language the classification is complete.

LetK,K′⊆ℝnK,K^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}be compact convex bodies. ThenK≡ℒaffK′K\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aff}}}K^{\prime}if

Theorem1.1is the affine half of a two-language picture.
The projective half we leave as a conjecture; the affine proof transposes only

## Conjecture 1.2(Projective categoricity).

LetK,K′⊆ℝnK,K^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}be compact convex bodies,n≥2n\geq 2. ThenK≡ℒBK′K\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}}K^{\prime}if and only ifKKandK′K^{\prime}are projectively equivalent.

The reconstruction behind Theorem1.1needs three things in
the projective setting (Proposition9.21): that
interior cross-ratios be first-order expressible
(Proposition9.7, proved in §9.2); that projective coordinates ofboundarypoints be recoverable from that interior data (done in the plane, open forn≥3n\geq 3); and that the body admit a compact family of gauge frames. The last
holds for polytopes and for bodies with compact projective symmetry, but fails
for the quadric, whose projective automorphism groupPO​(n,1)\mathrm{PO}(n,1)is
transitive on frames — so the quadric lies beyond reconstruction and must be
characterized positively (Conjecture9.17). None of these three
obstacles has an affine counterpart, which is why the affine case closes
outright. The polytope and ball theorems above are, in the affine language,
special cases of Theorem1.1; their projective analogues
(Proposition9.12, Conjecture9.17) sit under
Conjecture1.2.

The “if” directions of both statements are immediate from
Theorems3.1–3.4, since isomorphic
structures are elementarily equivalent. The content is the “only if”
direction: that the first-order theory of a standard body pins down its
affine (respectively projective) equivalence classamong standard
bodies. No stronger reconstruction is possible: by Löwenheim–Skolem
considerations the theory of a body always has nonstandard models
(Section11), so “categoricity” can only be meant
relative to the class of genuine convex bodies. For the affine language this is
achieved in Section8.

Three remarks on the shape of the conjecture and the methods. First, one
might worry that the moduli of a body — cross-ratios of vertex
configurations, say — are typically transcendental, while the parameter-free
definable real numbers of the ambient real-closed field are exactly the
algebraic ones, so that the theory could not “name” the moduli. This is a
red herring: a theory need not name an invariant to determine it. If for each
rationalqqthe comparison “the invariant exceedsqq” is expressible by a
sentence, then the theory records the full Dedekind cut of the invariant, and
distinct values are separated by a rational threshold.
Sections6,7and9are,
from this point of view, devoted to manufacturing such threshold sentences.
Second, the conjecture is consistent with, and illuminated by, the tameness
of the situation: for semialgebraic bodies the betweenness structure is
interpretable in the real-closed field, so its theory is decidable and its
definable sets are o-minimal; in particular no grid, lattice, or other
infinite discrete configuration is definable (Section5).
Third — and this is the principal structural tool beyond the plane — the
planar section of a body through three of its points is adefinableplanar convex body (coplanarity is a Radon-type betweenness condition), and
every first-order property of planar bodies can be relativized to sections
(Proposition4.8). Planar separations therefore propagate to
all dimensions, and the two-dimensional theory developed below is not a
special case but the engine of the general one.

On the projective side, the technical heart of the paper is
Theorem9.3: for collinear pointsa,b,c,da,b,c,din the interior of
a convex body inℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}, the harmonic relation(a,b;c,d)=−1(a,b;c,d)=-1is defined by a
betweenness formulaall of whose quantifiers range over the body. The
point is that the classical complete-quadrangle construction uses auxiliary
points that may escape the body; we show that the construction can always be
retracted into an arbitrarily thin neighborhood of the chord, inside a plane

so soundness needs no coplanarity hypothesis. This is the seed of an
“interior von Staudt calculus”: the constructions of the algebra of
throws, carried out without ever leaving the body, and hence available to the
first-order language. Carried to completion in Section9— a single interior perspectivity changes scale, and the harmonic net alone
generates every rational threshold — the calculus makes every
rational cross-ratio comparison definable, and yields — via the section
scheme — the separation of the closed unit ball from the quartic body{∑xi4≤1}\{\sum x_{i}^{4}\leq 1\}in the betweenness language, for everyn≥2n\geq 2, through
the Chasles–Steiner projective characterization of conics.

Finally, the betweenness language remains meaningful for noncompact convex
sets, and there the projective point of view produces genuine collapses: the
open orthant is betweenness-isomorphic to an open simplex, the slab to the
half-space, the open paraboloid region to the open ball, and the open solid
cone to an open half-cylinder (Proposition10.2). The spaceℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}, the slab, and the open ball are pairwise elementarily inequivalent,
separated — forn≥3n\geq 3— by sentences that test Euclid’s parallel postulate
on definable planar sections (Proposition10.6); the open
ball here is of course the Klein model of hyperbolic space. In the plane the
same trichotomy is witnessed by direct sentences
(Proposition10.5). We emphasize that forn≥3n\geq 3the
noncompact landscape is genuinely richer — cylinders, cones, and products of
lower-dimensional geometries appear — and its elementary classification is
left as an open problem.

## 8.Reconstruction from the extreme-point spectrum: the affine theorem

This section proves Theorem1.1: for compact convex
bodies,ℒaff\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aff}}-elementary equivalence coincides with affine equivalence in
every dimension. The argument has three ingredients. First, theℒaff\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aff}}-theory of a bodyKKdetermines theaffine spectrumof its

## 9.Interior von Staudt calculus in the betweenness language

In the betweenness language, midpoints and parallels are unavailable
(Example3.6); the projectively meaningful quantity is the
cross-ratio. The classical synthetic access to cross-ratio is von Staudt’s
calculus of harmonic conjugates and throws. The obstruction to importing it
here is that variables range overKKonly, while the auxiliary points of the
classical constructions — the vertices of a complete quadrangle, the centers
of pencils, the intersection points of Pascal’s theorem — routinely fall
outside the body. (For a convex hexagon inscribed in a circle, the three
Pascal points lie outside the disk; the “obvious” Pascal sentence is
thereforenota sentence about the structure of the disk.) The theme
of this section: the harmonic construction, and conjecturally the whole
calculus of throws, can be retracted into the interior of the body. All
constructions are planar; inℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}they take place in planes through the
relevant chord, and — as the soundness lemma shows — the defining formulas
force this of their own accord.

## Remark 10.7.

The open ball with its betweenness structure is the Klein model: its
section-geometry is uniformly hyperbolic, the slab’s is mixed
Euclidean/degenerate, andℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}’s is uniformly Euclidean —
Proposition10.6is the classical trichotomy read off from
the distribution of section geometries. More generally, each properly convex
open domain carries its Hilbert geometry, and theℒB\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}-classification of
such domains up to elementary equivalence contains the projective
classification problem for Hilbert geometries. Forn≥3n\geq 3the degenerate
(non-properly-convex) landscape — slabs, cylinders, cones, and products —
is genuinely richer than in the plane; see Example10.4and
Problem P8.

## Conjecture 10.8.

Conjecture1.2extends to open, properly convex
subsets ofℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}: two such sets areℒB\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}-elementarily equivalent if and
only if they are projectively equivalent.

## Remark 11.3(What reconstruction can mean).

Propositions11.1and11.2show that no
family of sentences — mesh sentences, cross-ratio cuts, or otherwise — can
recover a body up to isomorphism from its theory: the theory always has
non-archimedean and countable models in which, e.g., inscribed mesh patterns
are indexed by nonstandard integers. What the theory can aspire to determine
is the body’s affine (projective) equivalence classwithin the class
of standard bodies; that is Theorem1.1in the affine
language and Conjecture1.2in the betweenness language.
The affine aspiration is realized in full by Theorem8.6;
Theorems6.3and7.3are special cases.

## 12.Open problems
- P1.

(Conjecture1.2.) The affine case is settled:≡ℒaff\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aff}}}coincides with affine equivalence for all compact convex
bodies in every dimension (Theorem8.6), with the polytope
and ellipsoid theorems as special cases. The projective case — does≡ℒB\equiv_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}}coincide with projective equivalence? — is open, and
Proposition9.21isolates three obstacles, none with
an affine analogue: (a) interior cross-ratio expressibility, now settled
(Proposition9.7); (b) recovery of the projective
coordinates of boundary points from interior data, done in the plane
(Proposition9.12) and open forn≥3n\geq 3(Problem P7); (c) a
compact covariant family of gauge frames, which by the criterion
Proposition9.19exists exactly whenAutℒB​(K)\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}}(K)is compact — leaving as the only residue in (c) theℒB\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}-definabilityof such a family. In the plane (b) is discharged, and
for real-analytic positively curved non-conic bodies (c) is discharged as
well: the conic-cluster set (Definition9.28) is a finite
nonempty covariant set defined by pure incidence, so
Theorem9.31proves categoricity for this class
outright. The remaining planar cases are polygons (settled
via their finite vertex sets, Proposition9.12), the quadric (needing the positive
Chasles–Steiner treatment, Conjecture9.17), lower-regularity
bodies, and bodies with non-compact symmetry. Whether the quadric is the
only compact body with non-compact projective symmetry that resists
reconstruction — a question in the theory of divisible convex sets — is
itself open. Non-compactness ofAutℒB\mathrm{Aut}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}}alone is not the obstruction: the simplex has non-compact
projective symmetry yet is projectively rigid.
- P2.

Extend Theorem9.31beyond real-analytic
boundaries: forC∞C^{\infty}(or merelyC5C^{5}) positively curved non-conic
bodies, is the conic-cluster set still finite and nonempty? Finiteness is the
delicate point — a smooth non-conic curve can hyperosculate its conics on a
large zero set of the curvature derivative — while nonemptiness at a strict
curvature extremum of finite order follows from the proof of
Lemma9.29unchanged.
- P3.

(Question12.1.) Is the betweenness relation∅\emptyset-definable from the operations{Cλ}λ∈[0,1]\{C_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in[0,1]}over every compact convex body inℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}? A negative answer would separate
the pure barycentric language fromℒaff\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aff}}as used here; a positive answer
would make the inclusion ofBBinℒaff\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aff}}redundant.
- P4.

(Rigidity.) Call two compact strictly convex planar bodiesconfiguration-equivalentif for everyrrthey realize the same
distributions of projective invariants ofrr-point boundary
configurations. Does configuration equivalence imply projective
equivalence? For real-analytic boundaries the projective curvature is a
limit of configuration invariants, suggesting a positive answer there; the
general case is open, and by Proposition9.10a positive answer
(plus P2) would prove Conjecture1.2for strictly
convex planar bodies, whence — through the section calculus — leverage in
higher dimensions.
- P5.

Open-body analogues: is every open convex set inℝn\mathbb{R}^{n}ℒaff\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{aff}}-equivalent to the open ball an open ellipsoid? The boundary-based
sentenceβn\beta_{n}of Section7is unavailable; an interior
(limiting) form of the Bertrand–Brunn property is needed.
- P6.

Decidability beyond the semialgebraic: for which convex bodies
(subanalytic? with boundary definable in an o-minimal expansion of the
reals?) isThℒB⁡(K)\operatorname{Th}_{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}}(K)decidable, or at least tame in the sense of
Corollary5.2? O-minimality of the ambient structure yields
tameness of definable sets, but decidability requires an effective theory.
- P7.

Projective categoricity of polytopes inℝn\mathbb{R}^{n},n≥3n\geq 3: extend
Proposition9.12beyond the plane. The expected route is a
reconstruction of the projective type of the vertex configuration from
pencil cross-ratios measured on interior transversals within definable
planar sections; what is missing is a clean statement of which sectioned
invariants suffice.
- P8.

Classify the noncompact convex subsets ofℝn\mathbb{R}^{n},n≥3n\geq 3, up toℒB\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}-elementary equivalence: slabs, cylinders, cones, and products of
lower-dimensional geometries (Example10.4,
Remark10.3), and the Hilbert geometries of properly convex
domains (Conjecture10.8). Already the pair
(cylinder, slab) is open.
