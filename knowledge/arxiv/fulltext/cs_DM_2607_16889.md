## Abstract

The neighbourhood complexity $nc(G,k)$ of a graph $G$ is a quantity measuring, for a graph $G$ and an integer $k$, the maximum possible number (over all vertex subsets $S$ of size $k$) $|\{N[v]\cap S, v\in V(G)\}|$ of $S$-neighbourhoods in $G$. This notion is important in structural graph theory and algorithm design (especially in parameterized complexity, in particular model checking and kernelization).   While generally $nc(G,k)\leq 2^k$ and this bound can be achieved, it is known that sparse graphs and structured dense graphs have linear neighbourhood complexity, that is, $nc(G,k)\in O(k)$ for any such graph $G$. However, for many graph classes, the best possible constants are not known. We focus on graphs of bounded treewidth and pathwidth, showing that (when $k\geq w+1$) (i) if $G$ has treewidth $w\geq 2$, then $nc(G,k)\leq (k-w+1)2^{w}+w$, and (ii) if $G$ has pathwidth $w\geq 2$, then $nc(G,k)\leq (k-w+2)2^{w-1}+2k-w-2$. Moreover, we provide constructions that reach these bounds, whenever $w\geq 2$ and $k\geq 2w+1$ ($k\geq 2w-1$ for pathwidth).   Interestingly, in contrast, we also have the tight bound $nc(G,k)\leq \frac{7}{3}k$, for graphs $G$ with pathwidth 1 or treewidth 1.

## 1Introduction

The neighbourhood complexity of a graph is a notion that, informally speaking, quantifies the maximum possible number of neighbourhoods with respect to a given vertex subset. This notion is important in structural graph theory, as it can for example be used to characterize graph classes of bounded expansion[35].

Typically, graphs of bounded VC-dimension (such as geometric intersection graphs and sparse graphs) have polynomial neighbourhood complexity. While sparse graphs (with bounded expansion, planar, excluding a minor…) and structured dense graphs (of bounded clique-width, twin-width, or merge-width) have linear neighbourhood complexity[6,8].

Recently, the neighbourhood complexity has gained increased attraction, as it has proved to be a crucial tool for designing kernelization algorithms[20,24], model checking algorithms[17]and other parameterized algorithms[3,33]for structured graph classes, in connection with the study of structured graph classes and their properties[6,18,13]. In light of these important applications, it is relevant to obtain good (possibly tight) upper bounds on the neighbourhood complexity for significant graph classes.

Let us formally define neighbourhood complexity (we denote byN​[v]N[v]theclosed neighbourhoodofvv, i.e., the set of vertices ofGGat distance at most11fromvv).

## Definition 1(Neighbourhood complexity,nc\operatorname{nc}).

LetG=(V,E)G=(V,E)be a graph and letSSbe a set of vertices ofGG.
The neighbourhood complexity ofSS, denoted bync⁡(G,S)\operatorname{nc}(G,S), is the number of distinct intersections of closed neighbourhoods of vertices ofGGwithinSS:nc⁡(G,S)=|{N​[u]∩S,u∈V}|.\operatorname{nc}(G,S)=|\{N[u]\cap S,u\in V\}|.

Letkkbe an integer. We denote bync⁡(G,k)\operatorname{nc}(G,k)the maximum neighbourhood complexity over all vertex subsets of sizekkofGG:nc⁡(G,k)=maxS⊆V,|S|=k⁡nc⁡(G,S).\operatorname{nc}(G,k)=\max_{S\subseteq V,|S|=k}\operatorname{nc}(G,S).

## Connection to identification problems.

In the area of identification problems in graphs (and other combinatorial objects such as hypergraphs or discrete geometric structures), for a given combinatorial object, one wishes to select a (small) solution set, saySS(for example,SScould be a subset of vertices), so that all elements are uniquely identified by their relation toSS. For example, alocating-dominating setof a graphGGis a dominating setSSofGG(a set of vertices such that every vertex not inSShas a neighbour inSS) such that every vertex inV​(G)∖SV(G)\setminus Shas a unique neighbourhood withinSS[40]. Such a set is anidentifying codeif in fact, every vertex ofGGhas a unique (closed) neighbourhood withinSS[29]. In other words,SSis an identifying code if and only ifSSis a dominating set andnc⁡(G,S)=n\operatorname{nc}(G,S)=n, the order ofGG. The smallest size of a locating-dominating set ofGGis denoted byγL​(G)\gamma_{L}(G), while that of an identifying code ofGGis denoted byγI​D​(G)\gamma_{ID}(G); we will informally refer to these numbers as theidentification numbersofGG.

These types of problems have been extensively studied in the literature since the 1960s, under various names: see for example the works of Bondy[7]or Rényi[36]. We refer to the online bibliography maintained at[26], and to the book chapter[32], for more information and references.

It follows from the definitions that the number of vertices of a graphGG, with a locating-dominating setSS, is at mostnc⁡(G,|S|)+|S|\operatorname{nc}(G,|S|)+|S|. If furthermore,SSis an identifying code, thenGGactually has exactlync⁡(G,|S|)\operatorname{nc}(G,|S|)vertices.
Hence, upper bounds onnc⁡(G,k)\operatorname{nc}(G,k)immediately imply upper bounds on the number of vertices of graphs in terms of their identification numbers (by applying them to the special case wherekkis the identification number).
Conversely, the proofs upper-bounding the order of graphs in terms of their identification numbers, such as the ones in[4,9,11,21,22,34,41], can usually be translated into proofs upper-bounding the neighbourhood complexity of such graphs.

## Known bounds for various graph classes.

A notion close to that of neighbourhood complexity has been first considered for hypergraphs under the name oftrace functionorshatter function[1,23], defined analogously to neighbourhood complexity, but by replacing neighbourhoods by hyperedges. TheVC-dimensionof a graphGGis the size of a largest setSSsuch thatnc⁡(G,S)=2|S|\operatorname{nc}(G,S)=2^{|S|}[30], and a similar definition holds for hypergraphs, using the trace function instead of the neighbourhood complexity[42].

A classic result called theSauer-Shelah lemmaimplies that for (hyper)graphs of VC-dimension at mostdd, the trace function (neighbourhood complexity, respectively) is inO​(kd)O(k^{d})[38,39].

Typically, geometric graphs have bounded VC-dimension. For example, interval graphs have VC-dimension at most 2 and unit disk graphs have VC-dimension at most 3[9].
Planar graphs have VC-dimension at most 4, andKt+1K_{t+1}-minor-free graphs (which include graphs of treewidth at mosttt) have VC-dimension at mosttt[10,12]; more generally, graphs of bounded degeneracy have bounded VC-dimension[14].
Structured dense graphs, such as graphs of bounded rank-width (equivalently, clique-width)[10]or bounded MIM-width also have bounded VC-dimension.555The latter can be shown using similar arguments as in[28, Proposition 3.4].Thus, by the Sauer-Shelah lemma, all graphs in the above-mentioned classes have polynomial neighbourhood complexity.
Consult[9]for other graph classes of bounded VC-dimension.

Furthermore, sparse graphs typically have linear neighbourhood complexity. Indeed, for every graph class𝒞\mathcal{C}with bounded expansion, and for every graphGGin𝒞\mathcal{C}, we havenc⁡(G,k)≤f​(𝒞)​k\operatorname{nc}(G,k)\leq f(\mathcal{C})kfor some functionff.
In fact,𝒞\mathcal{C}has bounded expansion if and only if, for every positive integerrrand every graph in𝒞\mathcal{C}, itsdistancerr-neighbourhood complexityis at mostf​(𝒞,r)​kf(\mathcal{C},r)kfor some functionff[35](the distancerr-neighbourhood complexity can be seen as the neighbourhood complexity of therrth-power of the graph). For the more general graph classes𝒞\mathcal{C}that are nowhere dense, we have for some functionffand everyϵ>0\epsilon>0, for anyG∈𝒞G\in\mathcal{C},nc⁡(G,k)≤f​(𝒞,ϵ)​k1+ϵ\operatorname{nc}(G,k)\leq f(\mathcal{C},\epsilon)k^{1+\epsilon}[24].

Graph classes of bounded expansion include those excluding a fixed subdivision or a fixed minor, for example planar graphs, and graph classes of bounded treewidth. In the case of a planar graphGG, one can easily adapt the proofs from[34]for locating-dominating sets to show thatnc⁡(G,k)≤7​k−9\operatorname{nc}(G,k)\leq 7k-9, and moreovernc⁡(G,k)≤4​k−2\operatorname{nc}(G,k)\leq 4k-2ifGGhas treewidth 2, andnc⁡(G,k)≤7​k−12\operatorname{nc}(G,k)\leq\frac{7k-1}{2}ifGGis outerplanar. One can also easily deduce from bounds on locating-dominating sets of trees[41]thatnc⁡(G,k)≤3​k\operatorname{nc}(G,k)\leq 3kifGGis a forest.

Graph classes with bounded merge-width, recently introduced in[19], that form a very nice and general notion of structured dense graphs (or ”structurally sparse graphs”), also have linear neighbourhood complexity[6]. Such classes include graph classes of bounded expansion, graph classes of bounded twin-width, clique-width, etc. The case of graphs of bounded twin-width was studied in more detail in[8], where asymptotically tight bounds were provided.

## Treewidth and pathwidth.

Treewidth and its refinement pathwidth, in some general sense, measure how close the structure of a graph is to a tree or a path. For formal definitions, we refer to Section2. These graph parameters are among the most successful in structural graph theory, notably since the grand Graph Minor project by Robertson and Seymour[37], in which treewidth and pathwidth play an essential role. They are also very important for graph algorithms, notably due to the immense success of Courcelle’s “algorithmic meta-theorem” on Monadic Second Order Logic model checking for graphs of bounded treewidth[15]. See Chapter 7 in the book[16]for details on several algorithmic applications of treewidth, and[5]for a 2006 survey on the topic. More refined results exist for pathwidth, see for example[31].

Graphs of treewidth at mostwware sparse graphs, as they do not contain large complete graphs as minors and thus have bounded expansion. Hence, it follows that they have linear neighbourhood complexity[35]: at mostf​(w)​kf(w)kfor some functionff. There has already been earlier work focused on the neighbourhood complexity of graphs of given treewidth[2,27], however, it considered the distancerr-neighbourhood complexity of graphs of treewidthww, trying to determine the optimal asymptotic behaviour in terms ofrrandww. The current state of the art is an upper bound of2w+3​(w+1)w+1​(r+1)w+1​k2^{w+3}(w+1)^{w+1}(r+1)^{w+1}k[2]which gives forr=1r=1an upper bound of22​w+4​(w+1)w+1​k2^{2w+4}(w+1)^{w+1}k. A construction witnessingrr-neighbourhood complexity at leastΩ​(w−w​rw​k)\Omega(w^{-w}r^{w}k)is shown in[27], however note that it requiresr≥2w​(w+1)r\geq 2^{w}(w+1).

However, as we will see, this upper bound is far from tight.

## Our results.

We determine the best possible bounds on the (distance 1) neighbourhood complexities of graphs of given treewidth and pathwidth.

We start by proving an optimal upper bound on the neighbourhood complexity of graphs of treewidth 1 and pathwidth 1, which turns out to be the same bound. The method extends a proof from[4]for identifying codes of trees.

## 6Application to identification problems

We now state variations of our results for identification problems. For forests, it is known that a forest with a locating-dominating set of sizekkhas order at most3​k−13k-1and the bound is tight[41], and if it has an identifying code of sizekk, then it has order of at most⌊7​k3−1⌋\lfloor\frac{7k}{3}-1\rfloor, and this bound is also tight, even for caterpillars (that have pathwidth 1)[4].

For graphs of treewidth and pathwidth at least 2, almost identical proofs as in Section4can be applied to the setting of identification problems, leading to the following theorems.

## Proof.

We then fixkkandnnto be the maximum order of a graph with treewidthwwand a locating-dominating set of sizekk. Among all such graphs of ordernnwith a locating-dominating setSSof sizekk, we letGGbe one that has the smallest possible number of edges. The rest of the proof is exactly the same.

## 7Conclusion

We have determined tight upper bounds for the neighbourhood complexity of graphs of given treewidth or pathwidth and provided constructions attaining them. As further work, one could similarly determine optimal bounds for other classes of sparse graphs or dense structured graphs.

For the more general notion of distancerr-neighbourhood complexity, bounds inO​(f​(w)​rw+1​k)O(f(w)r^{w+1}k)andΩ​(rw​k)\Omega(r^{w}k)(forr≥2w​(w+1)r\geq 2^{w}(w+1)) are known for graphs of treewidthww, but the exact growth rate in terms ofwwandrris open[2,27]. Therr-neighbourhood complexity is also not fully understood for other types of graphs, such as, graphs of bounded twin-width[8]or planar graphs[27].
