# Shadow-Based Noise Fingerprinting of Simulated Quantum Noise Models

## Abstract

Accurate noise classification is essential for operating near-term quantum processors, yet existing approaches, such as quantum process tomography, scale exponentially with system size, limiting their practicality for routine calibration. We propose a measurement-efficient noise fingerprinting pipeline that combines structured classical shadow tomography with physics-informed feature engineering to identify noise channels from a fixed set of 3-qubit probe circuits. Each sample is represented by a 279-dimensional feature vector constructed from randomized Pauli measurements and derived observables, designed to resolve physically similar noise channels that produce overlapping signatures under generic measurement sets. We evaluate three classifiers, namely, random forest, extra trees, and a multilayer perceptron, on a dataset of 14,000 labeled samples spanning ten noise models. The random forest classifier achieves the highest test accuracy of 0.8426 with a macro F1 score of 0.8437, outperforming both baselines. Confusion analysis reveals that many noise models are classified with high reliability, with the remaining confusions occurring between channels sharing similar physical decay mechanisms, motivating future work on richer probe states and noise parameter estimation.

## IIntroduction

Quantum processors based on superconducting qubits and
trapped ions are rapidly advancing toward practical
applications, yet their performance remains fundamentally
constrained by hardware noise[16]. Noise in these systems arises from a variety of physical mechanisms,
including energy relaxation, dephasing, and measurement
errors, each of which degrades quantum information in
distinct ways. Understanding the dominant noise sources in a given device is therefore a prerequisite for effective error mitigation and the design of noise-aware
quantum algorithms.

Existing approaches, such as quantum process tomography[14], fully characterize noise processes but require resources that scale exponentially with system size, making them impractical for routine calibration of large-scale devices. Direct fidelity estimation and randomized benchmarking offer partial improvements in scalability, yet remain limited to specific noise models or rely on hardcoded decision rules that do not generalize across diverse error channels. As processors scale in qubit count and circuit depth, efficient and generalizable noise identification becomes increasingly critical[16].

In this work, we address this gap by proposing a noise
fingerprinting pipeline111Here, we use the term “noise fingerprinting” to refer to supervised classification of predefined noise-model families from shadow-derived measurement features. Our study focuses on simulated Qiskit noise channels and does not attempt full noise characterization or continuous noise-parameter estimation.that exploits the efficiency of
classical shadow tomography[6]to extract structured measurement data from a small set of 3-qubit probe
circuits[4]. Rather than relying on generic
observables, we construct a physics-informed feature
representation designed to amplify distinctions between
physically similar noise channels. We benchmark three
machine learning classifiers on ten candidate noise models
and demonstrate that ensemble methods substantially
outperform a neural baseline, achieving over 0.84 test
accuracy on a dataset of 14,000 labeled samples.

Our contributions are twofold. First, we propose a shadow-based noise fingerprinting pipeline using fixed 3-qubit probe circuits with a 279-dimensional physics-informed feature representation combining Pauli-shadow observables and derived coherence/population/asymmetry features. Second, we propose an empirical evaluation across ten Qiskit noise models and three classifiers, including confusion and scaling analysis. Our artifacts are publicly available athttps://github.com/AdaHgrace/Noise_Fingerprinting.

## IIBackground and Related Work

## II-ANoise models

Noise in noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices[16]arises from distinct physical mechanisms that can be modeled as quantum channels acting on the system’s density matrix[14]. We consider ten noise models spanning a broad range of error types: depolarizing, phase flip,
bit flip, readout error, phase damping, thermal relaxation, amplitude
damping, phase-amplitude damping, Pauli-asymmetric, and reset noise[14].
These range from symmetric decoherence channels to combined
energy-and-coherence loss models, providing a diverse and physically
realistic benchmark for noise fingerprinting.

## II-BShadow tomography

Shadow tomography predicts properties of a quantum state without requiring complete state reconstruction[1]. Classical-shadow protocols make this approach practical through randomized measurements and compact classical representations[6,17,7]. Extensions to noisy settings further support their use under realistic measurement conditions[9]. Their reduced measurement requirements motivate our use of shadow-derived observables as noise-sensitive features.

## II-CQuantum noise classification and characterization

Quantum noise has been studied through process tomography, fidelity estimation, randomized benchmarking, statistical fingerprints, and machine-learning analysis of measurement data[14,10,11,3,12,13]. Recent studies have also investigated efficient randomized-measurement and shadow-inspired representations for characterizing quantum systems and noise[17,5,2]. However, prior approaches generally estimate aggregate error properties, distinguish a limited number of noise processes, or learn device-specific signatures. Our work instead uses a common probe and shadow-measurement framework with physics-informed features to classify ten predefined noise-model families.

## IIIOur Method

Figure 1:Shadow-based noise-fingerprinting pipeline: probe-state preparation, noisy execution and randomized Pauli measurement, feature extraction, and machine-learning classification.

## III-APipeline overview

Our noise fingerprinting pipeline takes as input the measurement
outcomes from a fixed set of probe circuits executed on the noisy
device and outputs a predicted noise model from a set of ten
candidate models. The pipeline consists of four stages: 1) probe
state preparation, 2) randomized shadow measurements, 3) feature
extraction, and 4) machine learning classification, as illustrated
in Fig.1. At each stage, the design choices
are motivated by the need to extract discriminative
information from a minimal number of measurements, reflecting
the practical constraints of near-term quantum hardware. The
full pipeline is implemented in simulation using Qiskit[8]and evaluated on a dataset of 14,000 labeled samples distributed
equally across the ten noise models.

## III-BProbe state design

We use 3-qubit QAOA circuits[4]as complex probe states, owing to their ability to generate entangled states with rich coherence
structure that is sensitive to a broad range of noise mechanisms. QAOA circuits are parameterized by alternating cost and mixer layers, and in this work we use a single-layer ansatz with randomly sampled parameters to ensure diversity across probe instances. These complex probe states are supplemented with simple structured states, including computational basis states|0⟩⊗3|0\rangle^{\otimes 3},|1⟩⊗3|1\rangle^{\otimes 3}, uniform
superpositions|+⟩⊗3|+\rangle^{\otimes 3}, and Bell state(|00⟩+|11⟩)/2(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}on the first two qubits (with the third qubit initialized in|0⟩|0\rangle),
to capture
population-level, coherence-level, and correlation-based noise signatures. The combination of complex and simple probe states ensures that the feature extraction stage receives complementary information, improving the discriminability
of physically similar noise channels such as phase damping
and thermal relaxation.

## III-CFeature construction

Each sample is represented by a 279-dimensional feature vector
constructed from nine probe circuits, contributing 31 features per
probe circuit. The probe set consists of five QAOA probe circuits
with randomly sampled parameters and four simple structured states.
The first 18 features per probe are raw Pauli expectation values
estimated via the classical shadow protocol[6],
in which each probe circuit is followed by a randomly sampled
Pauli basis rotation prior to measurement, and expectation values
are reconstructed from the resulting bit strings over 200 shots.
We estimate nine single-qubit observables covering all three Pauli
axes on each of the three qubits, and nine same-axis 2-qubit
correlation observables covering all qubit pairs,
e.g.X1​X2​I3X_{1}X_{2}I_{3}andZ1​I2​Z3Z_{1}I_{2}Z_{3}.

The remaining 13 features per probe are physically motivated
derived quantities. LetX¯\bar{X},Y¯\bar{Y},Z¯\bar{Z}denote
the mean absolute response along each Pauli axis, and letM¯\bar{M}denote the mean absolute response over a mixed-axis
observable group. These include:
axis meansX¯\bar{X},Y¯\bar{Y},Z¯\bar{Z}, andM¯\bar{M};
coherence and population strengthsX¯+Y¯\bar{X}+\bar{Y}andZ¯\bar{Z};
pairwise differencesX¯−Y¯\bar{X}-\bar{Y},Z¯−X¯\bar{Z}-\bar{X},Z¯−Y¯\bar{Z}-\bar{Y}; and four
ratio featuresX¯+Y¯Z¯+ε\frac{\bar{X}+\bar{Y}}{\bar{Z}+\varepsilon},Z¯X¯+Y¯+ε\frac{\bar{Z}}{\bar{X}+\bar{Y}+\varepsilon},M¯X¯+Y¯+ε\frac{\bar{M}}{\bar{X}+\bar{Y}+\varepsilon}, andM¯Z¯+ε\frac{\bar{M}}{\bar{Z}+\varepsilon}.
The population-strength featureZ¯\bar{Z}duplicates theZZ-axis mean and is retained to match the evaluated implementation.
This design is motivated by the nearly indistinguishable signatures that physically similar channels, such as depolarizing and Pauli-asymmetric noise, produce under generic observable sets, necessitating physics-informed features to resolve fine-grained distinctions between noise models.

## III-DClassifiers

We benchmark three machine learning classifiers on the extracted feature vectors to assess whether model complexity is a limiting factor in noise model identification. The first two classifiers
are random forest and extra trees. Both methods construct ensembles of decision trees while considering random subsets of features. Random forest ordinarily fits trees to bootstrap samples, whereas extra trees adds stronger randomization by selecting candidate split thresholds randomly. The third classifier is an MLP with three hidden layers of 256, 128, and 64 units respectively, using ReLU activations and the Adam optimizer, included as a neural baseline to evaluate whether the added expressivity of a neural network provides any advantage over ensemble methods on this task.

All three classifiers are implemented using scikit-learn[15].
Except for the specified MLP architecture, classifier hyperparameters are left at their scikit-learn default values to provide
a controlled baseline evaluation, isolating the effect of
the feature representation rather than classifier tuning.

## IVResults

## IV-ADataset

We generated a dataset of 14,000 labeled samples distributed equally across ten noise models, yielding 1,400 samples per
class. Each sample corresponds to a single noise configuration applied to the fixed set of nine 3-qubit probe circuits. For each model, its primary error parameter is sampled uniformly from[0.01,0.15][0.01,0.15]; model-specific parameter mappings are provided in the artifact repository.
Feature extraction is performed, producing a 279-dimensional feature vector per sample from structured shadow measurements under 200 shots per probe circuit. The dataset is partitioned by stratified random splitting, ensuring equal class representation across all subsets. We use a 20% held-out test set of 2,800 samples; the remainder is split 85:15 into 9,520 training and 1,680 validation samples.

## IV-CConfusion analysis

Figure 2:Confusion matrix for the random forest classifier
evaluated on the held-out test set across ten noise models.

Figure2presents the confusion matrix for the random forest classifier evaluated on the held-out test set. Readout error, phase flip, bit flip, and thermal relaxation achieve high per-class accuracy, with the classifier correctly identifying the majority of samples in each of these categories. The primary misclassifications occur between phase damping and thermal relaxation, and between phase-amplitude damping and reset noise, suggesting that these channel pairs produce similar feature signatures under the current measurement protocol. The remaining noise models, including amplitude damping, phase-amplitude damping, and Pauli-asymmetric noise, achieve moderate accuracy, with errors spread across several physically related channels rather than
concentrated in one class.

## IV-DScaling analysis

Figure 3:Test accuracy as a function of samples per class for random forest, extra trees, and MLP classifiers.

Figure3presents classification accuracy as a function of the number of samples per class for all three classifiers. At 300 samples per class, the three models achieve accuracies ranging from 0.46 to 0.49, substantially above the 0.10 chance level. Accuracy increases consistently with dataset size across all classifiers. At 1,400 samples per class, random forest and extra trees reach accuracies of 0.8426 and 0.8406, respectively, while the MLP achieves 0.7925. The ensemble methods maintain comparable performance throughout the scaling curve and consistently outperform the MLP baseline at larger dataset sizes. The diminishing marginal improvement at larger dataset sizes suggests that the feature representation contains sufficient discriminative information for most noise classes, while distinguishing physically similar channels requires comparatively more training data.

## VDiscussion

## V-AAnalysis of classification performance

The high accuracy of the ensemble classifiers demonstrates that structured shadow measurements with physics-informed features provide a discriminative representation for most noise models. The confusion between phase damping and thermal relaxation is consistent with their shared coherence-decay mechanism, which produces similar expectation value profiles under the current probe and observable set. Similarly, the confusion between phase-amplitude damping and reset noise reflects the difficulty of distinguishing channels that produce overlapping effects on both population and coherence observables. The lower test accuracy of the MLP suggests that greater model expressivity alone does not improve performance. Further gains are therefore more likely to come from richer probes or observables than from more expressive classifiers.

## V-BLimitations

The current framework has several limitations that should
be acknowledged. First, the pipeline is currently limited to 3-qubit circuits. Extending it to larger systems would require redesigning the probe circuits and
observable set to capture additional single-qubit and multi-qubit noise signatures. Because the feature vector is constructed from probe-specific Pauli expectations and derived quantities, increasing the number of
qubits would also change its dimensionality and may increase the measurement and training requirements. We have not yet evaluated these larger-system designs, so their effect on classification accuracy, sample complexity, and generalizability remain topics for future investigation. Second, the pipeline does not yet support noise-parameter estimation,
providing only a discrete classification of noise model rather than a continuous characterization of noise strength. Third, three derived features associated with an unpopulated mixed-axis observable group were found to be constant across
samples, indicating a small amount of redundancy in the 279-dimensional feature space that future work could address with genuine mixed-Pauli observables. Fourth, all experiments are conducted in simulation. The simulations do not fully capture hardware-specific effects, such as state-preparation and measurement errors, calibration drift, crosstalk, and correlated noise. The reported performance therefore does not establish generalization from simulated noise models to physical quantum processors. Finally, the current probe circuits may not explore a sufficiently diverse set of quantum states to produce distinguishable signatures for all considered noise models affecting the QAOA circuits.

## VIConclusion and Future Work

This paper presented a shadow-based noise fingerprinting pipeline for classifying quantum noise channels from a fixed set of 3-qubit probe circuits. By combining randomized Pauli measurements with physics-informed feature engineering, the proposed method achieved 0.8426 accuracy and 0.8437 macro F1 across ten simulated noise models, with tree-based ensemble classifiers outperforming the neural baseline.

The results suggest that structured shadow-derived features can capture discriminative signatures for many common quantum noise channels while using a restricted set of shadow-derived observables rather than full process reconstruction. The confusion analysis further shows that remaining errors are concentrated among physically similar channels, indicating that the primary limitation lies in the separability of the current probe-feature design rather than classifier capacity.

Future work will extend the framework to larger quantum systems, incorporate noise-parameter estimation, evaluate robustness under real hardware noise and finite-shot variation, and explore probe designs that better separate physically overlapping channels.
