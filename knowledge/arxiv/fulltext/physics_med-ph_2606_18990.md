## Abstract

We present a novel multi-modal optical coherence photoacoustic elastography (OCPE) framework, which combines two imaging modalities, optical coherence tomography (OCT) and photoacoustic tomography (PAT), to enable complementary absorption-scattering measurements for the extraction of quantitative tissue features via quasi-static elastography. For this, we develop a sophisticated hybrid inversion algorithm for merging the complementary information layers contained in both OCT and PAT-based elastography measurements, and perform systematic evaluations to assess the impact of hybrid elastography data on strain and stiffness reconstructions. Studies on a silicone elastomer phantom demonstrate that the combined OCT-PAT approach outperforms single-modality OCT elastography and PAT elastography, yielding higher strain signal-to-noise ratio and improved stiffness estimates. These results establish the advantage of multi-modal complementary imaging and data merging for accurate, high-resolution elastographic strain and stiffness mapping in both scattering and absorbing materials.

## Abstract

We present a novel multi-modal optical coherence photoacoustic elastography (OCPE) framework, which combines two imaging modalities, optical coherence tomography (OCT) and photoacoustic tomography (PAT), to enable complementary absorption-scattering measurements for the extraction of quantitative tissue features via quasi-static elastography. For this, we develop a sophisticated hybrid inversion algorithm for merging the complementary information layers contained in both OCT and PAT-based elastography measurements,
and perform systematic evaluations to assess the impact of hybrid elastography data on strain and stiffness reconstructions. Studies on a silicone elastomer phantom demonstrate that the combined OCT-PAT approach outperforms single-modality OCT elastography and PAT elastography, yielding higher strain signal-to-noise ratio and improved stiffness estimates. These results establish the advantage of multi-modal complementary imaging and data merging for accurate, high-resolution elastographic strain and stiffness mapping in both scattering and absorbing materials.

## IIntroduction

Elastography is a non-invasive imaging modality, which aims at mapping the mechanical properties, such as the stiffness, within a sample, using conventional depth-resolved imaging techniques[63,49,64,62,48]. In term of diagnostics accuracy, one is interested in quantitative values of stiffness mapped on top of the visualization of a sample, rather than only in qualitative images. Depending on the technique, elastography can quantify liver[51]and thyroid[75]stiffness, characterize breast lesions[3], investigate changes in cornea stiffness induced by glaucoma[63]and can even be integrated into endoscopes[70,43].

Medical elastography has been used in clinical practice for about 25 years[62,49]. Commercial elastography systems, employing ultrasound (US) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are routinely used to diagnose medical conditions such as liver fibrosis, cancerous thyroid nodules, and breast lesions[15,20]. While both US and MRI elastography perform well on the scale of whole organs, their resolution is insufficient to resolve micrometer-sized features and therefore, they cannot detect stiffness inhomogeneities at that scale. However, achieving higher imaging resolution is particularly desirable for early cancer detection. Optical coherence elastography (OCE)[63]and photoacoustic elastography (PAE)[61]have been proposed to enable finer-detail stiffness imaging. Both OCE and PAE have shown promising results in clinical research, most notably in breast cancer detection[35,44].

In this work, we present quantitative stiffness and strain reconstruction results from jointly acquired OCT and PAT data for elastography. We refer to this imaging modality asoptical coherence photoacoustic elastography(OCPE). We propose amulti-layered hybrid inversion algorithmfor OCPE, which leverages dual-modality inputs to quantitatively estimate mechanical properties of the sample. The proposed reconstruction method is adapted to the key features of optical coherence tomography (OCT) and photoacoustic tomography (PAT) by first extracting their complementary information layers from the imaging data and then solving the prior-regularized inverse problem. The hybrid elastography data are essentially fused by the hybrid inversion algorithm based on feature tracking, segmentation, an optical flow strategy, and a regularized inverse problem of parameter estimation type. In overall, this work presents an algorithmic and experimental framework for implementing OCPE as an imaging modality and investigates its current performance for quantitative imaging. The reconstruction results in SectionVIdemonstrate the advantage of multi-modal OCPE versus single-modality OCE and PAE on phantom studies. OCPE not only enhances the information density available for the stiffness reconstruction, but also addresses the limitations of the individual imaging modalities. When applied separately, OCT is prone to shadow artefacts: highly scattering or absorbing features attenuate the incident light, reducing intensity at depth and producing shadows. In contrast, PAT is less sensitive to this effect, and can contribute the missing information. PAT visualizes only absorbing features, which are often sparsely distributed in biological samples, e.g., blood vessels in soft tissue. OCT fills these gaps by providing information-rich morphological structure; see Fig.1for motivation. The quality of the elastographic stiffness reconstruction depends strongly on the information contained in the underlying images, making the combination of the complementary imaging modalities, PAT and OCT, particularly valuable in this context. In addition, our proposed approach is not limited to unidirectional displacement along the imaging axis, as most OCE methods are[33], but also accounts for lateral displacement, leading to more precise and comprehensive results, as we have shown previously[36].

## IIOn hybrid imaging modalities

OCE achieves high-resolution stiffness mapping at high imaging speeds, and has been extensively studied in the literature, see the reviews[63,74]. It is based on optical coherence tomography (OCT), a technique that scans a sample with a laser and uses interferometry to detect backscattered light, producing three-dimensional, depth-resolved morphological images. A wide variety of OCE approaches, differing in loading mechanism, strain detection, and target application, have been proposed. Loading can be performed, e.g., by compression[36,57], air puffs[68], optical excitation[16], vibrations[34], or endogenously by the heartbeat[63]. OCE strain detection schemes commonly leverage either the image intensities (intensity-based OCE)[36,57]or the phase of the recorded signal (phase-based OCE)[63,33]. Applications range from cancer margin detection[35], to intravascular elastography[10,12], and stiffness assessment of skin lesions[21]or the cornea[63].

PAE is a recently developed application, based on photoacoustic (PA) imaging (PAI)[64]. Conceptually, PAI records the acoustic response of a sample to short laser pulses: it uses the photoacoustic effect to map sample features that absorb at the excitation wavelength. Two distinct PAI approaches have been developed, namely photoacoustic tomography (PAT)[23]and photoacoustic microscopy (PAM)[31]. In this work, we employ PAT due to its greater penetration depth compared to PAM, which offers higher spatial resolution. Both modalities are commonly used to visualize blood vessels[50]. The photoacoustic intensity[24]and the properties of the photoacoustic waves themselves[47]can be employed for stiffness reconstruction. PAE has been successfully demonstratedin vivoin the female breast[44], skeletal muscle[25]and blood vessels in the human finger[26], among others.Figure 1:Motivation for multi-modal imaging: Dual‑modal OCT-PAT imaging of a cancer biopsy reveals complementary internal structure, providing information not available from either modality alone. a) OCT; b) OCT and PAT image combined; c) PAT. Note that while the combination of PAI and OCT represents a novel approach in elastography, it has been successfully employed in conventional multi-modal imaging, for instance forin vivoimaging of human skin[13], mouth ulcers[54], Barrett’s oesophageal neoplasia (using a prototype edoscope)[42], and organoids[17].

The publication[71]reports to perform multi-modal elastography with OCT and PAM. The data from both modalities is used for feature learning to differentiate major anatomical regions of the mouse brain. However, neither qualitative nor quantitative stiffness maps are provided, raising questions about the classification of[71]as elastography. In[72], the connection between PA signal and stiffness is leveraged to map the elasticity and viscosity of mouse brain samples via conventional signal analysis. The tissue attenuation is additionally mapped using OCT, yielding a multi-modal technique that relies on a single modality for elastography. In[16,41], the PA effect is used to generate surface waves, which are detected by OCT, and analyzed to estimate the tissue stiffness solely based on information derived from OCT without acquiring PA images. The combination of PAI and OCT has been shown to be beneficial in various other research settings[46]. Depending on the employed technology, the PAI and OCT images are either readily co-aligned[42,40], or require additional co-registration steps[13]. The co-registration is typically performed using rigid or elastic body transformations, guided by features visible in both modalities[13]. In summary, neither of the above publications fully leverage hybrid, complementary OCT and PAT measurements to derive mechanical parameters quantitatively, which is the main contribution of this work.

## IIIRelated Work

To the best of our knowledge, no prior work combines information from more than a single imaging modality to derive elastograms. The terms ‘dual-modal elastography’ and ‘multi-modal elastography’ are commonly used to describe elastography as another information layer in addition to one or more imaging modalities. In such studies, however, only a single imaging modality is used as input for the elastographic reconstruction, which is then added as ‘another modality’. Although a few publications feature photoacoustics, OCT and elastography, e.g.,[71,72,16,41], none of them leverage dual-modality data inputs to estimate mechanical properties of the sample, which constitutes the novelty of this work.

Quantitative elastography with hybrid data belongs to the class of inverse problems with internal measurements[69], in which one has access to interior functionals of the solution within the domain in addition to or instead of boundary data. Hybrid imaging techniques have been proposed to overcome the severe ill-posedness of many classical inverse problems that are typically based on boundary data. Internal data transform these problems into mildly ill-posed or even locally well-posed ones[2,5,38,37,1]. Coupling two or more physical modalities allows to indirectly observe interior quantities, which leads to improved uniqueness and stability in reconstructions[5]. A general theoretical framework for treating hybrid inverse problems in a unified way has been developed in[5]. The experimental realization of coupled-physics imaging techniques requires novel hybrid methods with internal data[38]. Compared with[69,2,5,38,37,1]and the references therein, this work is transitional, serving as a bridge between theoretical and practical aspects of quantitative elastography with internal data, illustrated on OCPE. In various elastography techniques, imaging modalities provide interior measurements of displacement or strain within the medium[18]. Multiple measurements in elastography can further enhance identifiability and stability[69]. However, in practice, experiments can be limited to a single uniaxial deformation applied from one direction, unlike the theoretical assumptions. In our experimental setting, we compensate for the lack of additional measurements by extracting the flow field of sample features indicating the general internal internal motion in the initial steps of our hybrid algorithm. We adapt an optical flow strategy to incorporatepriorinformation for estimating the internal data. The final steps of the proposed hybrid algorithm are based on an inversion of the internal data within a parameter identification inverse problem.

In this section, we present our developed reconstruction techniques that are an integral part of our proposed workflow enabling multi-modal OCPE.

First, we provide a brief description of the workflow from the perspective of input data and reconstructed quantities. We consider a quasi-static elastography experiment, in which the sample is deformed by an externally applied load. The sample is imaged before and after deformation, following a purposefully designed scanning routine (see SectionV-Bfor details). The acquired imaging data serve as the primary input to our elastography framework, which aims to quantitatively estimate thestrainandstiffnessvalues within the sample. This can be achieved by following one of two main approaches to quantitative elastography; see Fig.2. The first and most common one, is known as thetwo-step approach, and involves the consecutive solution of two inverse problems: in inverse problem 1 (IP1), the displacement or strain inside the sample/tissue undergoing deformation is estimated from its imaging data; in inverse problem 2 (IP2), the material parameters of the sample/tissue are then reconstructed from the obtained displacement or strain estimates. ## IV-AStrain

In the rapidly evolving field of OCE, numerous methods for computing strain have been developed over the years[63]. The two prevailing directions of OCE leverage either the phase sensitivity of OCT imaging systems or conventional cross-correlation techniques of reflectivity-based speckle in the medium[63]. However, the new multi-modal elastographic OCT-PAT data pose additional challenges for these strain estimation approaches. The PAT acquisition time restricts the deformation experiment to quasi-static loading and comparably large compressions, thus currently excluding the use of phase-sensitive OCT. Moreover, adding absorption contrast to a previously reflectivity-based OCE workflow undermines conventional cross-correlation and requires alternative handling. Consequently, jointly acquiredreflection-absorption datacall for new processing and new displacement and strain estimation techniques.

In this section, we focus on the estimation of the internal displacement field𝐮\mathbf{u}and strainℰ​(𝐮)\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{u})that develop within the sample during the compression experiment, i.e., solving IP1. It is important to note that the reliability of stiffness estimates in two-step approaches depends on the accuracy of the displacement and strain.

Hence, we here propose a noveldata‑fusing elastographic optical flow method (DEOFM)that combines optical coherence and photoacoustic imaging data algorithmically to yield high‑quality displacement and strain maps. This method builds upon our previously developed displacement and strain evaluation technique[60,36,59]for OCE, whose distinguishing feature is the availability of both lateral and axial strain components, unlike conventional OCE or phase‑sensitive OCE variants. The EOFM approach, when applied to the straightforward fusion of the OCT and PAT scans described above, is sensitive to the choice of the brightness weightζ\zetaand to the strategy used for combining (e.g. superimposing) the scans. EOFM attempts to fit a single update field to both OCT and PAT images simultaneously, without distinguishing their modality-specific information. The method therefore misfits parts of the estimated displacement field due to the OCT shadowing artefacts and the invisibility of the highly absorbing structures, and to the lack of information about the reflective background medium in PAT. These modality-specific features are not accounted for by EOFM. Hence, we now propose a split-step modification of EOFM to overcome its shortcomings in application to multi-modal data. We introduce a modality-dependent split step of the update calculation. The proposed DEOFM approach merges OCT and PAT data at the level of the functional formulation, i.e., by fusing both modalities within a single objective functional. First, under a homogeneous medium assumption (as previously), we compute an initial guess𝐮bg\mathbf{u}^{\text{bg}}, and then search for an update𝐮upd\mathbf{u}^{\text{upd}}of the displacement field using OCT data and feature tracking information, thereby reducing the influence of the shadows and highly absorbing structures. We then use the full displacement field from OCT data, obtained by adding the background and the update fields, as an initial background guess for the next step. The new update field is then estimated using PAT data to leverage the more clearly visible absorbing structures of the sample. Finally, the full displacement field𝐮\mathbf{u}is obtained by adding the background𝐮bg\mathbf{u}^{\text{bg}}and update𝐮upd\mathbf{u}^{\text{upd}}fields.

## IV-BStiffness

In this section, we summarize the reconstruction techniques we use to estimate the stiffness (i.e., Young’s modulus) values within the sample from acquired OCT-PAT imaging data, following the one-step and two-step approaches to quantitative elastography as described in the introduction to SectionIV. In both cases, we have to solve a so-called inverse problem of parameter estimation type: given observations (data), find the causes of the phenomenon (parameters). In elastography, this means that we are interested in finding the unknown material parameters, given observations of the material behavior, by solving the inverse problems IP2 or IP3, see Fig.2and Fig.3. Note that both inverse problems are ill-posed[4,7,6,18]and thus sensitive to even small amounts of noise in the data. Hence, one must carefully choose methods for obtaining (approximate) solutions to these inverse problems and understand their limitations. We discuss the reconstruction methods for IP2 in SectionsIV-B1,IV-B2, and for IP3 in SectionIV-B3.

## IV-B1Direct Strain Inversion Method

In a quasi-static elastographic experiment, the internal stress distribution is unknown and cannot be measured directly. In case of uniaxial compression, a uniform stress is commonly assumed in order to estimate Young’s modulus from the strain maps[63]. This assumption ignores the inclusion’s effect on the stress field, as well as any boundary influences. The applied compression force recorded during the experiment is divided by the contact area between the sample and the compression plate to obtain the uniform value of stressσ\sigma. Then, following the general Hooke’s law (5), a simplified inversion formula for estimating the Young’s modulusE​(x)E(\textbf{x})from the strain mapℰ​(x)\mathcal{E}(\textbf{x})at each pointx(or each pixel, in the discrete case) writes asE​(x)=σ/ℰ​(x),E(\textbf{x})=\sigma/\mathcal{E}(\textbf{x})\,,(8)

where the strainℰ​(x)\mathcal{E}(\textbf{x})is calculated using (3) from the displacement field provided by our DEOFM algorithm from SectionIV-A. This reconstruction approach is called direct strain inversion (DSI).

## V-DData Processing

OCT and PAT images are recontructed from their respective raw datasets using standard reconstruction algorithms. A k-Wave-based algorithm is used for PAT[66]and a fast Fourier transform (FFT) with standard background subtraction for OCT[19,22]. The OCT image is rotated to ensure that the top and bottom surfaces of the sample (i.e., the imaging window and the back compression plate) are orthogonal to the image’s depth axis. This step is not necessary for PAT, since the FPI is fixed orthogonal to the interrogation beam. Afterwards, PAT and OCT pixel sizes are resampled to match dimensionally. OCT images exhibit depth‑dependent attenuation, which is a natural effect caused by scattering in superficial layers of the sample. However, it can constitute a challenge for motion estimation methods based on OF. This problem is mitigated by applying a depth-dependent intensity compensation. Since the lateral phantom boundaries are approximately parallel to the OCT illumination beam, this leads to refraction-induced artefacts in the region. Consequently, we restrict the analysis to a region of interest (ROI) within the phantom, excluding the boundary artefacts.Figure 8:Segmentation: Results of PAT image segmentation at compression steps 1 (cs1) and 6 (cs6), shown as yellow inclusion outlines overlaid on OCT (top) and PAT images (bottom). For details on the selected automated segmentation algorithms see SectionV-D.

Next, we perform OCT-PAT co-registration. For this purpose, fiducial markings are applied on the back compression plate with a permanent marker. These markings are clearly visible in both imaging modalities and thereby facilitate co-registration. The absorbing inclusions within the phantom are prominent in PAT, since they contribute the dominant contrast. They are also faintly visible in OCT, appearing as low-signal voids. This set of features, identifiable in both modalities, provides the basis for a rigid body transformation that is used to align the PAT and OCT images. Finally, the PAT images are cropped laterally to the same ROI as the OCT images. Then, in axial direction, data above the glass window and the back compression plate are removed. To locate the back compression plate, we use a gradient‑based search on OCT images that identifies pixels at the gradient discontinuity and computes their mean position for finding the cutting plane.

For DEOFM analysis, we generate a 2D maximum intensity projection (MIP) from the 3D ROI for both PAT and OCT by selecting central slices and leveraging the experiment’s symmetry to map the cylindrical inclusions to a circle and a bar. This step is necessary, since there is an inherent ambiguity in 3D motion determination via OF. We cross-check symmetry by analysing the 3D sparse displacement fields from feature tracking (see SectionV-E), searching for symmetry planes in the data. ## VIResults & Discussion

As noted in SectionV-B, we present our strain and stiffness results for deformation between the compression steps 1 (cs1) and 6 (cs6), which fall within the linear elastic regime.

## VI-AStrain

First, we reconstruct the strain from OCPE imaging data via DEOFM (SectionIV-A) and compare it with strain maps from single-modality OCE and PAE estimated via EOFM. All reconstructions were performed in Python on a Linux workstation (24 ×12th Gen Intel®Core™i9-12900K, 62.6 GiB of RAM) with an average wall‑clock runtime of 3-5 minutes per reconstruction. The resulting axial and lateral strains are visualized in Fig.11and13, and already clearly show the benefits of our novel multi-modal elastography approach.
OCE detects the regions of lower axial strain within the phantom, but strongly overestimates the inclusion strain. PAE is limited to the information available from PAT. The PAT signal is strongest on the side of the absorbing inclusion that is closest to the excitation (see Fig.7). This intensity gradient, together with the missing background information, results in an incomplete mapping of the inclusion strain. The zoom region insets in Fig.11and Fig.13illustrate the strain maps with an adaptive color scale, indicating that the inclusions are indeed visible in OCE and PAE. Benefiting from higher information density, OCPE is capable of reconstructing a more accurate and realistic strain distribution, as shown in Fig.12. Across all results in Fig.13, we observe that the inclusion oriented parallel to the imaging plane is not visible in the lateral strain maps. We attribute this effect to the stiff inclusion acting as lateral constraint that inhibits lateral deformation in the surrounding material. It is well known that strain analysis is hindered by imaging artefacts. Both OCT and PAT can exhibit modality-specific imaging artefacts. In particular, strong absorbers or scatterers reduce the light intensity available for imaging in deeper regions of the sample, leading to a ‘stripy’ appearance of the OCT image. In the most severe case, known as shadow artefacts, the deeper regions of these vertical stripes are completely dark, preventing the acquisition of morphological information in these areas. PAT can exhibit similar shadow artefacts, but they are generally much less pronounced. In FPI-based PAT, more severe artefacts arise from the photoacoustic image reconstruction. The FPI is flat, positioned on one side of the sample, and detects only the part of the sound waves which is perpendicular to its surface. Consequently, only a limited portion of the photoacoustically generated sound waves is recorded. The reconstruction artefacts originating from this lack of information are negligible in anen faceview of the 3D data. In a transverse view, which is important for elastographic analysis, the artefacts become more pronounced and appear as ‘wing-shaped’ features (see Fig.8).
Taken together, these artefacts pose challenges for motion reconstruction techniques based on optical flow. From OCPE strain and imaging data, we reconstruct the sample’s stiffness using the three different inversion techniques described in SectionIV-B: direct strain inversion (DSI), nonlinear Landweber iteration (NLI), and the intensity-based inversion method (IIM). IIM, which solves a reduced-dimensionality minimization problem, relies on PAT segmentation and derives stiffness estimates at specified inclusion locations (Fig.16). The obtained stiffness values for the inclusions and background are summarized in Tab.III, where the mean values are calculated over regions defined by the PAT segmentation masks (SectionV-D) for the DSI and NLI reconstructions. In SectionVI-C, we discuss how stiffness evaluation is influenced by the projection direction of the data, the size of the inclusion, and its position relative to the compression and imaging planes.

DSI performs worst overall, underestimating the background stiffness by19.8kPa19.8\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}and the inclusion stiffness by429.2kPa429.2\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}on average. As described in SectionIV-B, DSI assumes a constant stress distribution within the sample. This assumption holds only if the stiffness is homogeneous and the boundary of the sample can be neglected. Neither condition is satisfied in our deformation experiment. The impact of the stress inhomogeneity caused by the inclusions is greatest in the inclusion region, which explains why the inclusion stiffness is determined inaccurately, while the background stiffness could be calculated rather precisely. In general, the homogeneous stress assumption should be used with caution in elastography, since it implies that stiffness inhomogeneities are quantified while simultaneously assuming their absence in the data.

As can be observed from the reconstruction result in Fig16, the two-step NLI is able to accurately recover the structure of the sample. However, it struggles to retrieve the correct contrast: NLI overestimates the background stiffness by88.0kPa88.0\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}and underestimates the stiffness of inclusion 1 by281.1kPa281.1\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}and inclusion 2 by134.1kPa134.1\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}. The NLI approach does not require a constant stress assumption and the relevant information is expected to be encoded in the strain (i.e., in Fig11and Fig.13). Hence, the reconstruction quality of NLI depends directly on the strain maps used as the input of IP2. Since the strain first has to be estimated from the sample images by solving IP1, the quality of this estimate depends on the imaging data and the chosen strain evaluation method. Mathematically, IP2 (as well as IP3) requires more than one displacement or strain field to ensure a globally unique and high-quality reconstruction of spatially varying stiffness; see[7,39,4]for details. Since our experiment is limited to a single deformation (uniaxial and from one direction), we compensate for the lack of additional measurements by using feature tracking information. This complementary information in the mathematical formulation allows us to achieve the current level of reconstruction quality.

To mitigate this limitation and improve stiffness estimates, we rely on dimensionality reduction of the problem and avoid computing derivatives from the noisy data (i.e., the calculation of strain from the displacement field in IP2). For this, we solve IP3 by applying the IIM approach directly to the OCT-PAT imaging data and leverage previously obtained PAT segmentation. Fig.15and Tab.IIIillustrate that IIM performs best among all reconstruction methods: IIM overestimates the background stiffness by40.5kPa40.5\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}and underestimates the stiffness of inclusion 1 by265.6kPa265.6\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}and inclusion 2 by only39.8kPa39.8\text{\,}\mathrm{k}\mathrm{P}\mathrm{a}.

Both strain-based methods (DSI and NLI) fail to produce reliable stiffness reconstructions, due to a lack of structural features and the limited quality of the OCE and PAE strains. Image-based IIM is robust only when the necessary priors are available. We observed that OCT data without PAT-based segmentation cannot reliably solve the OCE minimization problem since the problem is severely ill-posed. Similarly, PAE alone suffers from a lack of OCT structural information; even when PAT segmentation is available, the problem remains ill-posed. In both OCE and PAE, the local minimizers lie outside of the admissible parameter set (i.e., realistic stiffness constraints). Conversely, the complementary OCT-PAT data contain the necessary prior information for the robust implementation of quantitative elastography. As mentioned above, we consistently observed that all inversion methods underestimate the stiffness of inclusion 1, although it should have the same stiffness as inclusion 2; see Tab.III. We infer that stiffness estimates are influenced by the orientation of the imaging data, the inclusion size and its position relative to the compression and imaging planes. To investigate this, we performed several reconstruction tests using IIM on two orientations and two deformations of the sample (cs1-cs6 and cs1-cs7), within the linear elastic regime (see Tab.I). Fig.17illustrates the 3D sample with inclusions marked by numbers, and the 2D MIP x- and y-orientations of the 3D OCT data volume. Notably, the same inclusion appears different in size and shape, depending on the x- or y-orientation. As seen in Fig8, the inclusion brightness is depth dependent relative to its location to the imaging plane. Furthermore, the inclusion is subject to different displacement when it is near or far from the compression plate. This has important implications for inverse problems: since the object is probed via an applied force, small or negligible loading (i.e., in the regions far from the compression plate) reduces the observability of stiffness variations and hinders the performance of inversion methods. Note also that the stiffness estimation inverse problem (IP2) requires more than one linearly independent displacement or strain field to ensure a globally unique, high-quality reconstruction. In addition, the problem unavoidably accumulates various errors in the data, such as discretization errors, 3D to 2D projection errors and post-processing error. Taken together, these factors (inclusion area, location, exposure to deformation, image intensity) may affect the stiffness estimates. To support these our assumptions, we performed uncertainty quantification for the two test settings with respect to variations in the Poisson’s ratio and OCT-PAT image brightness (see Fig.9). The variation step size was set to0.0050.005for both parameters. The estimated uncertainties and the distribution of IIM solutions are displayed in two charts in Fig.17. The results show that the background stiffness is only slightly affected by parameter variations or by the choice of orientation and compression test. We observe a significant improvement in the stiffness estimates for inclusion 1, when the IIM input data are switched to the y-orientation. We attribute this to the larger area of inclusion 1. The stiffness estimates for inclusion 2 remain consistently within the same range across all tests. The most precise reconstruction results are obtained for compression test cs1-cs7 with the x-orientation, reaching the ground truth values.

Finally, another contributing factor may be the structure of our phantom: two stiff beam-like, perpendicular inclusions are embedded in a soft background with an approximate stiffness ratio of 1:6.9 (see Tab.III). These stiff inclusions may act as longitudinal (along the inclusion) constraints, inhibiting transverse deformation in the surrounding material and influencing the deformation of both inclusions[27,9]. In particular, inclusion 2, which is closest to the compression plane, may strongly inhibit transverse deformation and influence the reconstructed Young’s modulus value of inclusion 1.

## VIIConclusion

In this paper, we introduced anovel multi-modal optical coherence photoacoustic elastographyapproach, termed as OCPE, which for the first time combines both OCT and PAT measurements for the extraction of quantitative tissue features via quasi-static elastography. We described the OCPE framework and presented our developed multi-layered hybrid inversion algorithm to estimate the strain and to quantify the Young’s modulus within a sample. We investigated OCT-PAT data-fusion techniques, and assessed their impact on the quality of strain and stiffness reconstructions. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the OCPE approach outperforms single-modality OCE and PAE on phantom studies, see in particular Fig.11and Fig.15. Reconstructions were validated against the ground truth values determined with a commercial device, see Tab.III. These results establish the advantage of multi-modal absorption-scattering OCT-PAT imaging and data merging for robust, quantitative elastography.

She received her PhD in Medical Physics at the same institution. She also holds a diploma in Biomedical Engineering from the Technical University of Vienna. Her current research focuses on quantitative imaging based on optical coherence tomography and photoacoustic tomography, with a special interest in elastography. She is also interested in visualising pathological vascularity changes by means of optical coherence tomography angiography.Wolfgang Drexleris a full professor and head of the Center for Medical Physics and Biomedical Engineering at Medical University of Vienna, after having been a full professor of biomedical imaging at Cardiff University, Cardiff, Wales, UK, from 2006 to 2009.
