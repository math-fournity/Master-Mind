## Abstract

The Discrete Fourier Transform, the Discrete Cosine Transform, and their block-wise variants underpin most deployed image and video codecs. Their effectiveness rests on three properties: they run in near-linear time (linear up to a polylogarithmic factor), they are exactly invertible, and they carry few to no parameters. In this work, we generalize these bases to isometric multilinear bases, allowing a small number of extra parameters, polylogarithmic in the image size, while preserving all three properties. Given an image dataset, we develop a systematic framework that searches this family for the basis compressing the dataset most effectively: the basis is parameterized as an isometric tensor network, inspired by quantum many-body theory, and trained with Riemannian optimization on the manifold of unitary matrices. Across natural photographs and line drawings, the trained bases consistently improve on their fixed, non-parametric counterparts. On Quick Draw line-drawing compression, they store images in roughly $20\%$ fewer bytes than JPEG's $8 \times 8$ block cosine transform at the same reconstruction quality.

## 1Introduction

Transform coding sits at the core of most deployed image and video codecs, from the Joint Photographic Experts Group (JPEG) standard[1]to High Efficiency Video Coding (HEVC)[2]. A fixed, fast transform maps image blocks or prediction residuals to coefficients, most of the signal energy lands in a few of them, and these coefficients are then quantized and entropy-coded[3,4]. How well the transform concentrates energy sets how much the later stages can compress, so the transform is an important design choice in the pipeline. For decades this role has been filled by fixed trigonometric transforms. The most prominent are the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)[5]and its block-wise variants, together with the closely related Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT), whose fast algorithm, the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)[6], supplies the template for computing such transforms quickly.

No fixed basis, however, serves every image dataset equally well. A fixed basis has no parameters, so it cannot adapt to any of them. For example, the DCT comes close to the optimal Karhunen–Loève transform only for sources near the first-order autoregressive Gaussian regime[7]. Natural photographs sit near this regime; line drawings do not. To adapt the basis to the data, methods such as deep autoencoders[8,9]replace the fixed transform with trainable nonlinear encoding and decoding maps. These learned transforms usually compress better, but they cannot take over the role of the traditional transforms, because the maps themselves carry far more parameters than a single image. A usefulsparse basis, one whose coefficients concentrate an image’s energy in a few entries, should keep the properties that make the DCT and the FFT so effective:
- 1.

Negligible parameter overhead: the basis is described by a number of parameters that is negligible compared with the image size.
- 2.

Fast to apply: the transform and its inverse run in linear time, up to a factor polylogarithmic in the image size.
- 3.

Exactly invertible: the inverse map is explicit, so the transform is lossless before truncation.

Under these constraints, we seek a systematic scheme that learns a basis for a given dataset, so that the dataset as a whole shrinks at the cost of storing only a negligible number of basis parameters.

Our insight comes from quantum computing, which supplies a precise dictionary between images and quantum states. An image withN=2nN=2^{n}pixels, flattened into a length-NNvector, has exactly the shape of annn-qubit state vector.
A quantum circuit composes unitary gates into a reversible linear map, so from the image’s point of view a circuit is simply a change of basis. Simulating a circuit ofpoly​(log⁡N){\rm poly}(\log N)gates exactly takesO​(N​poly​(log⁡N))O(N\,{\rm poly}(\log N))time on a classical device, near-linear in the image size. The FFT itself is such a circuit, the quantum Fourier transform[10]: contracting its gates one at a time costsO​(N​log2⁡N)O(N\log^{2}N), and fusing the gates level by level recovers theO​(N​log⁡N)O(N\log N)cost of the classical FFT.
A quantum circuit is also mathematically equivalent to a tensor network[11,12], a multilinear map in which every tensor is constrained to preserve norms. For simplicity, we therefore parameterize the image basis as an isometric tensor network[13,14]rather than as a quantum circuit[15,16,17,18].
What remains open is which network topology (the connectivity pattern of the tensors) best fits a given class of images, how to train it, and how the trained basis depends on the dataset. In the worst case, neither of the first two questions has an efficient exact answer. Finding the best tensor decomposition structure is NP-hard[19], and so is training the parameters of even a fixed circuit[20]. We therefore settle both empirically.Figure 1:Overview of the framework. In this paper, we first recast the traditional FFT, DCT, and their block variants as isometric tensor networks.
We then propose a family of parameterized isometric tensor networks as image bases, several of which directly extend these standard transforms. We train them per dataset with Riemannian optimization[21,22], that is, gradient descent performed directly on the manifold of unitary matrices, and show that the trained bases consistently outperform their fixed counterparts.
On natural photographs (DIV2K[23]), whose statistics the cosine basis already fits well, the trained basis matches the DCT. On non-photographic data such as Quick Draw line drawings[24,25], it stores each image in roughly20%20\%fewer bytes at the same reconstruction quality.

## 3Isometric tensor networks as sparse bases

In order to find the best isometric tensor networkT​(θ)T(\theta)’s structure, we realize a familyℱ\mathcal{F}as tensor networks, whose small decomposed isometric tensors act directly on the binary digits of the image indices. An image𝐱∈ℝ2m×2n\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{m}\times 2^{n}}has one row and one column index. Writing both indices in binary recasts𝐱\mathbf{x}as a tensor withm+nm+nbinary legs, drawn as one open wire per qubit,𝐱\mathbf{x}. A basisT​(θ)T(\theta)is then an ordered contraction of small isometric tensors, each tensor acting on one or two legs. Applying it amounts to attaching these gates to the image block𝐱\mathbf{x}and absorbing them one at a time. The contraction order is fixed by the network’s recursion rather than optimized, and fusing its levels gives theO​(N​log⁡N)O(N\log N)schedule derived insectionA.3. The construction begins with the classical fast transforms, since the FFT already has exactly this network form.

Its recursion produces the sparse real circuit infig.2e; relaxing those gates within manifolds preserves orthogonality and retains the exact DCT-IV as its initialization.SectionA.2derives the recursion, bit reversal, and gate-wise relaxation.

## 3.2Quantum variants and complexity

The remaining topologies vary the wiring and the local gate, following circuit patterns from quantum many-body physics.Entangled QFTkeeps the two QFT registers and adds one controlled phaseMMMMbetween each matched row–column pair. Setting the phases in these entangled pair gates to zero recovers the separable QFT.TEBD[28],MERA[29], andRichBasisshare a single gate set, theU​(2)U(2)single-qubit layer with unrestricted two-qubit tensors inU​(4)U(4), and differ only in how those tensors are wired.TEBDuses a nearest-neighbor ring,MERAa hierarchy of disentanglers and isometries, andRichBasisfurther relaxes the QFT’s phase gate to the all-to-all pattern.

Fixing the shape ofT​(θ)T(\theta)fixes the product manifoldℳ\mathcal{M}it optimizes at. QFT and Entangled QFT useU​(2)U(2)andU​(1)4U(1)^{4}; TEBD, MERA, and RichBasis useU​(2)U(2)andU​(4)U(4); DCT-IV usesO​(4)O(4),O​(2)O(2), andU​(1)4U(1)^{4}.Table3lists each exact tensor beside the family it relaxes to, andsectionA.3defines more rigorously these manifolds.

EveryT​(θ)T(\theta)in the family preserves the requirements ofsection2. Because every local tensor is isometric, the composed transform is unitary and can be inverted by reversing the gates. At mostO​(log2⁡N)O(\log^{2}N)fixed-size gates describe any topology, so the parameter overhead remains polylogarithmic. With the family of isometric tensor network basis constructed, the next question naturally becomes how we could train theseT​(θ)T(\theta)efficiently.

## 4Training the parametric basis

With image sparse bases constructed as isometric tensor networksT​(θ)T(\theta), we use Riemannian optimization to find the parameters that minimize the reconstruction error ineq.1, shown inFigure1d. Training alternates a lossy reconstruction pass with a geometry-aware update of the gates insideT​(θ)T(\theta). Riemannian optimization guarantees that every optimization step returns each gate to its manifold, so the tensor network remains isometric throughout training.

## 4.3Initialization and optimization scope

Initialization is topology-specific: QFT, RichBasis, and DCT-IV begin at their defining fixed-transform values, while the other architectures use seeded feasible parameters. Because the objective is non-convex, these choices do not provide a global-optimality guarantee, and randomly initialized parameterized circuits are known to face flat optimization landscapes[37,38]. The gate-unfreezing, random-seed, and DCT-IV perturbation studies inappendicesCandDinstead test empirical stability over the initializations and schedules considered there. Training holds out15%15\%of the training slice as a validation set and returns the parametersθ⋆\theta^{\star}with the lowest validation loss; the test images play no role in this selection. The resulting transformT​(θ⋆)T(\theta^{\star})is stored once as the dataset’s shared basis and used for compression and reconstruction infig.1e.

## 6Conclusion and discussion

## 6.1Conclusion

We introduced a continuous parametric family of unitary image bases, obtained by reading the classical fast transforms as isometric tensor networks and relaxing each gate within its matrix manifold. Every member is unitary by construction, applies inO​(N​log⁡N)O(N\log N)time, and carries a parameter count polylogarithmic in the image size, and the family contains the FFT and the DCT-IV as exact points. One basis is trained per dataset with Riemannian optimization, minimizing the reconstruction error from the retained top-kkcoefficients, and the trained transform is then stored and applied like any fixed fast transform.

The experiments support two conclusions. Across both datasets the decisive design choice is the local gate manifold rather than the wiring, with the full-tensor bases leading the diagonal-phase pair at every keep ratio from0.050.05upward. Against the classical references, the gain tracks how far the source sits from the AR(1)–Gaussian regime where the cosine basis is near optimal: the trained bases match the DCT on natural photographs and beat it clearly on line drawings, where the byte-level codec stores each image in roughly20%20\%fewer bytes at matched quality.

## 6.2Discussion

The connectivity axis is close to saturated. Wiring the sameU​(4)U(4)tensor as a ring, a hierarchy, or all-to-all moves DIV2K by at most0.90.9dB and Quick Draw by at most0.30.3dB at any ratio from0.050.05upward, whereas exchanging that tensor for the diagonalU​(1)4U(1)^{4}phase costs2.52.5dB on DIV2K and5.85.8dB on Quick Draw atρ=0.20\rho=0.20. Further gains must therefore come from priors the family does not yet encode or from the optimization loop itself.

The clearest missing prior is an explicit block structure. JPEG’s8×88\times 8cosine transform owes part of its robustness to exact block boundaries, which confine quantization errors and keep the per-block coefficient budget small. A blocked variant of the family would wrap each trained circuit in the same prior while preserving unitarity, the parameter count, and the near-linear cost, and block-transform coding experience[39]suggests it should help on block-structured content. We report no blocked variants, so this is future work rather than a result. The prior also has a known cost, since content whose dominant correlations span the frame favors full-image transforms; a per-dataset or per-image choice of block size, and ultimately a learnable block structure, is the natural end point of this line.

On the optimization side, the objective can move beyond hard top-kktruncation. The loss ofeq.1is blind to the ordering within the retained set, the failure mode behind the RichBasis drop atρ=0.01\rho=0.01, and it never sees the quantizer or the entropy coder that the codec ofsection5.2applies afterwards. Smooth surrogates for the selection step, objectives averaged over several keep ratios, and rate–distortion losses evaluated on the coded byte stream would each train the basis against the quantity the benchmarks report. Training cost has similar room. The batched evaluation ofsectionB.3contracts the gates in their fixed circuit order, so each step pays the one-tensor-at-a-time cost ofsectionA.3. Fusing recursion levels, or optimizing the contraction order outright[40,41], would move the per-step cost toward the fast-transform schedule and admit larger registers and deeper topologies.

Two limitations bound the result. Each basis is trained offline for one dataset, so the method is a basis-design tool rather than a deployable codec, and the topology is fixed in advance, with the blocked and objective-level variants above left to future work. ## B.3Batched evaluation

The transform is never materialized as a dense2m+n×2m+n2^{m+n}\times 2^{m+n}matrix. A2m×2n2^{m}\times 2^{n}image is reshaped intom+nm+nbinary legs, one per qubit, andT​(θ)​𝐱T(\theta)\,\mathbf{x}is evaluated
by contracting the network of fixed-size gate tensors against these legs,
with every gateGpG_{p}kept as a separate contraction factor. Hadamard-role
gates contract one leg each, while diagonal gates reuse the wire labels of
their control and target legs and introduce no new legs. The contraction
order is fixed by the topology rather than searched, and in particular it is
not delegated to JAX’s defaulteinsumpath policy. The gates are
applied one at a time in their circuit order, each as an axis-wise
contraction against the image tensor, with diagonal gates entering as
broadcast multiplications, and the fixed sequence is compiled once per
topology with JAX[48]and cached. Its gate count and arithmetic
cost are therefore the one-tensor-at-a-time schedule ofsectionA.3. The adjointT​(θ)†T(\theta)^{\dagger}reuses
the same network with conjugated gate tensors and reversed gate legs, so
the forward and inverse transforms share one parameter set.

A minibatch adds one batch leg. The per-image lossℒk\mathcal{L}_{k}of
(1) is vectorized over the leading batch axis
with the gate tensors broadcast across it, and the training objective is
the batch mean. Reverse-mode differentiation through the compiled
contraction[49,50]therefore returns one accumulated ambient gradient per gate,
which is exactly thegtg_{t}consumed by the update ofsectionB.1. Keeping every gate as a separate contraction
factor preserves its independent manifold constraint; gates that share a
factor manifold are grouped and updated by one stacked
projection–retraction kernel.

This batched evaluation matches the implementation in the open-sourcepdftpackage used for all reported experiments, which fuses the
loss, the accumulated per-gate gradients, and the gate-wise Riemannian Adam
update ofsectionB.1into a single compiled training step.
