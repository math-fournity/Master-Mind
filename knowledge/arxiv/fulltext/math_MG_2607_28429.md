## Abstract

For an $n$-dimensional convex body $K$, let $θ_L(K)$ denote its lattice covering density. We prove that there is an absolute constant $C>0$ such that, uniformly over all such bodies, \[   θ_L(K) \le Cn\log n\,(\log\log n)^{10/3+o(1)}. \] The $o(1)$ term is independent of $K$. This improves the previous universal bound $O(n^2)$ of Ordentlich, Regev, and Weiss and, together with the classical linear lower bound of Coxeter, Few, and Rogers for Euclidean balls, determines the optimal polynomial growth exponent of the worst-case lattice covering density.   The main innovation is a vertical--horizontal construction that converts a uniform weight estimate on the full Boolean cube into a lattice covering of any convex body. In a low-dimensional vertical space, folded Gaussians and a finite-field Kakeya theorem select, for every translate, a Boolean pattern with total normalized weight $d^{-5/2+o(1)}$. Gaussian marginals transfer the resulting weights to sections of the body, while entropy yields one horizontal lattice with a very small uncovered set. Finite-index lifting then assembles all shifts into a single lattice, and Rogers' completion lemma makes the covering exact.

To find the useful heights, we fold a narrow Gaussian into the unit cube. Each base point of the cube determines Gaussian weights on its Boolean vertices. The folding identity separates the total weight of the cube from the way this weight is divided among the vertices. The entropy estimates then show that no single vertex receives too much weight and that vertices with very small weights can be removed while retaining almost all of the total.

Good base points must be available above every vertical translate. We select aninformation layeron which the total weight and the entropy bounds are controlled, and divide the unit cube into a finite-field grid. The high-rank Kakeya theorem of Kopparty, Lev, Saraf, and Sudan[14]gives a linear subspace such that every grid cell can be shifted by an element of this subspace into the layer. Construction A lifts the subspace to a lattice inFF. It follows that every vertical translate contains a Boolean cube satisfying the required entropy bounds and having total normalized Gaussian weightd−5/2+o​(1)d^{-5/2+o(1)}; seeSections˜3.3and3.4.

We then relate these Gaussian weights to the geometry ofKK. After placingKKin isotropic position, the pointwise marginal theorem of Eldan and Klartag[7]allows us to chooseFFso thatvolm⁡(Ky)\operatorname{vol}_{m}(K_{y})is approximatelyvoln⁡(K)\operatorname{vol}_{n}(K)times the standard Gaussian density atyythroughout the relevant range. Thus the Gaussian weights describe the relative volumes of the corresponding sections. A finite discretization replaces these sections by finitely many slightly smaller convex bodies. We keep their individual volumes instead of shrinking them all to the same size. Ifρ\rhois the target covering density, their total volume above every vertical translate, divided by the horizontal lattice determinant, isρ​d−5/2+o​(1)\rho d^{-5/2+o(1)}.

The remaining task is to cover these finitely many, differently sized sections by one horizontal lattice. The mean-hole estimates of Rogers[19]and Schmidt[22]provide a lattice for which each retained section leaves only a small uncovered set. Shannon entropy justifies removing the sections with very small weights, while min-entropy keeps every remaining section within the range of the mean-hole estimate. We then choose horizontal shifts so that the uncovered proportions multiply. A finite-index shear incorporates all these shifts into one lattice inE⊕FE\oplus F. The two parts of the proof refine different earlier ideas. Ordentlich, Regev, and Weiss[15]used a finite-field Kakeya theorem to turn an almost-covering statement into one valid for every translate. We use the same correction principle only in the small vertical spaceFF, and we choose the rank together with the information layer rather than fixing it in advance. Gao, Liu, Pikhurko, and Sun[10]used Boolean vertices to amplify lattice coverings of Euclidean balls. We use the same multiplication effect, but the sections of a general convex body have unequal volumes. The weighted formulation and the finite-index shear are what allow the amplification step to work in this setting.
