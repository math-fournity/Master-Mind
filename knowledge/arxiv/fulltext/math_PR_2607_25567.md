## Abstract

We start by surveying known properties of first passage percolation (FPP) geodesics on a Gromov-hyperbolic group $G$. Due to a coalescence phenomenon established in earlier work of the authors, a random tree $T(ξ,ω)$ consisting of infinite random geodesics to a point $ξ$ on the Gromov boundary $\partial G$ emerges naturally. These trees have a rich random geometry that we explore further in this paper.

## 1.Introduction

First passage percolation (FPP) is a well-studied model of a random metric on a graph, obtained by assigning independent and identically distributed random lengths to each edge. Over the last 60 years, FPP has been extensively studied, primarily on Euclidean background geometry. Of late, there has been interest in studying FPP on different background geometries, in particular on Gromov-hyperbolic graphs[BT17,BZ12,BM22,BM26]. This partly expository paper starts by surveying known theorems for FPP on Cayley graphs of Gromov hyperbolic groups, primarily the results established in[BM22,BM26]. An important fact established in[BM22]is coalescence of random geodesics directed towards a common pointξ\xiin the Gromov boundary∂G{\partial G}ofGG: given a hyperbolic groupGGand
for everyξ\xiin the Gromov boundary∂G{\partial G}ofGG, any two geodesic rays (in the random first passage percolation metric) directed towardsξ\xialmost surely coalesce. As observed in[BM26], for almost every realizationω\omegaof the random environment, the collection of FPP geodesics directed towardsξ\xiforms a random treeT​(ξ,ω)T(\xi,\omega), referred to in that paper and below as thebackward random treein the directionξ\xi.

Such geodesic trees are commonly studied for first and last passage percolation models onℤ2\mathbb{Z}^{2}. We refer the interested reader to the excellent monograph[ADH17]for details on what was known classically, and to[Hof05,Hof08,DH14,DH17,AH]for recent progress in planar Busemann functions.
It was established by Damron and Hansen in[DH14, Theorem 1.10(4)]that, under mild conditions, “backward clusters” are finite. For finer properties in the case of the exactly solvable exponential last passage percolation, see[BBB23].
One of the motivations for studying this in the context of hyperbolic FPP is to study the effect of the ambient geometry on such random trees. We showed in[BM26], that under mild conditions on FPP on hyperbolic groups and for a fixedξ∈∂G\xi\in{\partial G}, the backward random treeT​(ξ,ω)T(\xi,\omega)is almost surelyinfinite in all directions, i.e., for everyξ′≠ξ\xi^{\prime}\neq\xi,T​(ξ,ω)T(\xi,\omega)contains a bi-infinite geodesic(ξ′,ξ)ω(\xi^{\prime},\xi)_{\omega}. After reviewing the relevant results from[BM22,BM26], the rest of this paper is devoted to further study of the random treesT​(ξ,ω)T(\xi,\omega).

## Organization of the paper

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We start off in Section2by recalling the basic set up of first passage percolation on hyperbolic groups and by describing the results about the first order and fluctuation behavior of the FPP metric from[BM22]. In Section3, we recall the relevant results about direction and coalescence of infinite geodesics in hyperbolic FPP established in[BM22,BM26]. The remaining sections contain some new results about the trees and some related statistics. In Section4we show that the point of coalescence between two random geodesics in the same boundary direction is typically close to the deterministic centroid of the three endpoints. As a consequence, we show (see Corollary5.3) that the backward sub-trees ofT​(ξ,ω)T(\xi,\omega)rooted at far enough points along a horosphere are contained in disjoint cones with large probability. In Section5.2, we study the growth of the backward trees, and show that the backward subtrees are finite with positive probability, and study a few related questions.
In particular, we
reprove a slightly weaker version of[BJQ25, Theorem A]in Corollary5.16.

## 6.2.Future Directions

General geometries:One natural and interesting direction is to investigate how much of the theory developed for the FPP on hyperbolic groups can be extended to more general settings. As was remarked in[BM26], many results about geodesics, bi-geodesics and their coalescence can be easily extended to general hyperbolic graphs under mild conditions, whereas the law of large numbers result in Theorem2.5crucially depends on the group structure. It would be interesting to see, if these results can also be proved in a continuum setting, such as FPP models based on a Poisson process defined on the hyperbolic plane. It would also be interesting to explore FPP models on negatively curved spaces without using the full power of hyperbolicity. It was proved in[BT17]that bigeodesics exist in FPP on hyperbolic graphs with a Morse bi-infinite quasi-geodesic. This result has recently been extended to the case ofsublinearly Morsequasi-geodesics in[JQ25].


CLT and fluctuations:Recall that in Theorem2.5we showed that the passage times grow linearly with a fixed velocity in almost every direction, whereas in Theorem2.11we showed that the variance of the passage time between two points grows linearly in the distance between them. Given this, it is natural to conjecture (and in fact, it was conjectured in[BM22]) that the passage times, centered and scaled by the mean and standard deviation, respectively, converge weakly to a Gaussian distribution. A central limit theorem answering this question has recently been proved by Chawla and Gorski[CG25]. It is of course a very interesting question to try and understand the fluctuation and scaling limit for FPP on more general groups and graphs, and decipher connections between the geometry of the ambient space and the fluctuations of the FPP metric. Recall that for FPP onℤ2\mathbb{Z}^{2}, the variance between two points at distancennis expected to grow liken2/3n^{2/3}(see[ADH17]), and to prove this remains one of the most important open questions in the area.


Exceptional directions and N3G:Finally, we discuss questions regarding exceptional directions for geodesic trees. Recall from Theorem3.17that ifGGis not virtually free then exceptional directions exist both for the forward geodesic trees (the treeF​(v,ω)F(v,\omega)consisting of the semi-infinite FPP geodesics starting from a fixed pointvv), as well as the backward geodesic trees (the treesT​(ξ,ω)T(\xi,\omega)consisting of the coalescing geodesic rays in a fixed boundary directionξ\xi). Consider the set of exceptional directions in the forward geodesic treeF​(v,ω)F(v,\omega)for a given realizationω\omegaof the FPP metric.

## Question 6.4.

Are these exceptional directions independent of the base-pointvv?

This question is related to the behavior of exceptional directions with respect to theG−G-action. A result showing that exceptional directions are independent of the base point was proved in[JRAS23]in the context of the exactly solvable model of planar exponential last passage percolation. Another interesting direction of research is to understand the multiplicities of exceptional directions. Recall from Theorem3.17, (4) and (6), that the maximum number of disjoint geodesics in an exceptional direction is at leastdimt∂G+1\dim_{t}{\partial G}+1whereas we only have a non-explicit absolutely constant upper bound depending on theGGand the passage time distribution. It is interesting to understand whether the lower bound is tight, i.e.

## Question 6.5.

Is it true that almost surely there are no exceptional directions with
multiplicity greater thandimt∂G+1\dim_{t}{\partial G}+1?

Following a general conjecture from[CFT23](see also[Cou11,JRAS23]for a proof in the context of planar exponential LPP) it is expected that the lower bound is indeed tight for planar hyperbolic FPP (the case covered in Theorem3.17, (3)). Thus one expects that almost surely there are no exceptional directions with multiplicity33or more. In an ongoing joint work with Chawla, we are studying the no three geodesics (N3G) phenomenon in planar hyperbolic FPP, but the question remains out of reach in higher dimensions.
