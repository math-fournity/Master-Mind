## Abstract

Sampling from multimodal distributions is a longstanding challenge for classical local Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods. A popular remedy is to introduce a sequence of intermediate distributions that interpolate between the target and a simpler reference. The classical choice, tempering, raises the density to a power, but distorts the relative weights of asymmetric modes and can lead to poor mixing. We instead propose interpolating along the diffusion path, the marginals of a noising diffusion process that carries the target toward a Gaussian. This path preserves the relative weights of the modes and enjoys favorable mixing properties, which we make precise through a spectral-gap analysis of the corresponding ideal transition kernel. Sampling along the path requires its intermediate scores, which can be estimated from the unnormalized target through variational approaches, yielding only an approximate sampler. To remove the resulting bias, we introduce the Metropolis-adjusted diffusion path (MAD-Path) sampler, which corrects the diffusion-path proposal in an augmented path space and leaves the target invariant regardless of the accuracy of the learned score or the discretization error. We further quantify how these two errors affect the acceptance probability, providing guidance for practical tuning. Experiments on a range of Bayesian posteriors show that MAD-Path improves global exploration and mode-weight estimation relative to tempering-based MCMC methods and unadjusted diffusion samplers.

## 1Introduction

Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is the standard framework for sampling from a density known only up to its normalizing constant, and it serves as the computational engine of Bayesian statistics and many scientific applications. The most widely used algorithms—random-walk Metropolis[RWM;45], Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm[MALA;60], and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo[HMC;23,49]—generate proposals through local moves, and consequently mix poorly when the target has several well-separated modes, as the chain only rarely crosses the low-probability barriers between them. Such multimodal targets arise routinely in Bayesian posteriors, through label switching in mixture models[37], parameter constraints[65], and nonidentifiability[22], as well as throughout the sciences; seeŁatuszyńskiet al.[40]for a recent survey.

A general strategy for sampling from multimodal distributions is to introduce a path of intermediate distributions that connects the targetppto a flatter distribution on which the separated modes are easier to traverse. The classical construction is tempering: for a decreasing sequence of inverse temperatures1=β0>β1>⋯>βn>01=\beta_{0}>\beta_{1}>\cdots>\beta_{n}>0, one considers intermediate densities proportional topβkp^{\beta_{k}}. Asβk\beta_{k}decreases, differences in log-density are compressed, reducing the energy barriers between modes and allowing local Markov transitions to move more freely across regions that are nearly disconnected under the original target. This idea underlies simulated tempering[43,25], parallel tempering[66,26], and tempered transitions[47], among other methods. Closely related annealing paths also form the basis of annealed importance sampling[48]and sequential Monte Carlo samplers[20].

Tempering can, however, fail when the local modes exhibit asymmetry. For instance, when the modes differ in scale, raising the density to a power distorts their relative weights. Consider an equal-weight mixture of two well-separated Gaussians with different scales. Under the tempered densitypβp^{\beta}withβ<1\beta<1, the narrower mode has a weight that is exponentially small in the dimension. This weight distortion is known to cause torpid mixing of simulated and parallel tempering for asymmetric mixtures, with spectral gaps that vanish exponentially in dimension regardless of the temperature schedule[74]. A related phenomenon has been described in the learning-based sampling literature as “teleportation of mass”: when the tempering interpolation is viewed as a curve in Wasserstein probability space, probability mass is transported nonlocally along the path, leading to an irregular velocity field[44,15]. Weight-preserving variants of tempering[69,68]target this issue directly, but they require knowing the location and scale of every mode and therefore do not resolve it in general.

This shortcoming of tempering motivates the search for a better interpolating path. Crucially, most of the algorithms above—simulated and parallel tempering, tempered transitions, and their relatives—do not depend on the tempering path in particular; they require only a sequence of intermediate distributions whose unnormalized densities can be evaluated, and any such sequence may be substituted. A particularly attractive alternative is to interpolate at the level of the samples rather than by powering the density. The difficulty in sampling with the diffusion path is that the intermediate distributions are intractable. To transport a Gaussian sample to the targetpp, one must run the noising diffusion backward in time, with a drift that involves the score∇log⁡pt\nabla\log p_{t}at every intermediate time[4]. This reverse-time construction is the basis of score-based generative modeling[61,62,32], where the scores are estimated from data by denoising score matching.
In generative modeling, diffusion models have become a leading class of flexible samplers. Their success is due not only to the expressiveness of neural networks for estimating the score, but also to the structure of the diffusion path itself: the forward process gradually transforms a complex data distribution into noise, so that generation can be formulated as a sequence of simpler denoising problems rather than a single global transport problem. This is closely analogous to the role of annealing paths in tempering-based MCMC[61].

In the sampling setting, however, one has no samples fromppand only access to its unnormalized density. Recent work therefore estimates the intermediate-time scores through stochastic optimal control or path-space variational formulations[77,57,71]. The resulting diffusion sampler is only approximate: its output distribution is affected by score-estimation error, finite-time truncation of the diffusion path, and numerical discretization[16]. Moreover, recent empirical studies suggest that successful applications to multimodal targets often require incorporating the target score∇log⁡p\nabla\log pdirectly into the score parametrization; without this information, the learned sampler can suffer from severe mode collapse[10,31]. This is costly, however, since the target score must then be evaluated at every discretization step. Mode collapse can be alleviated further by encoding the target’s mode structure into the reference distribution, for instance, by locating the modes from preliminary samples[50]. But even then, the sampler can remain inaccurate when the target has a strongly nonlinear score that is hard to approximate. We return to these practical aspects of score learning in Section5.

Unlike other approximate samplers, such as normalizing flows, diffusion-based samplers do not provide a simple bias-correction mechanism. A normalizing flow is a deterministic invertible map from a tractable reference distribution, so the change-of-variables formula gives the exact density of each generated sample. This density can then be used to debias the sampler by importance weighting or a Metropolis–Hastings correction.
This convenience, however, comes at a price. First, requiring an invertible map with a tractable Jacobian determinant greatly constrains the expressiveness of normalizing flows[53]. Second, in dimension two or higher, infinitely many diffeomorphisms carry one density to the other, and it is unclear which one a normalizing flow should learn. Diffusion-based samplers, in contrast, place no invertibility constraint on the parametrization, and because they prescribe the entire marginal path, the object to be learned is uniquely determined.
The tradeoff is that samples are generated through a stochastic path, and the marginal density of the endpoint is generally intractable. Although it can be evaluated through the associated probability-flow ODE[62], this requires solving an additional ODE and, in practice, introduces numerical discretization error. Thus, diffusion-based samplers do not come with exact density evaluation, making their sampling bias difficult to remove by standard correction methods.

To address this difficulty, we propose an MCMC algorithm that uses diffusion paths as proposals and corrects the resulting bias with a Metropolis–Hastings step. In each transition, the current state is first evolved forward along the noising diffusion path, then back along an approximate reverse path. This forward-then-backward construction mirrors the heating-then-cooling structure of Neal’s tempered transitions[47], with the diffusion path in place of the tempering path. Near the noisy end, the target’s modes are smoothed together, so the chain can move between them freely; the reverse path then transports the state back towardpp. Because the proposal is a stochastic trajectory, the Metropolis–Hastings correction is applied to the entire path in an augmented space. This augmented-space correction ensures that the marginal chain hasppas its exact stationary distribution, regardless of the accuracy of the learned score or the size of the discretization error.

The main contributions and the outline of the paper are as follows.
- •

In Section2we describe the ideal transition kernel induced by the diffusion path and study its mixing, first when the target satisfies a Poincaré inequality and then when it is a mixture of such distributions. This ideal kernel coincides, up to a rescaling, with the proximal sampler[41,17]. Our analysis takes a new perspective, interpreting the proximal sampler as a data-augmentation Gibbs sampler. This lets us bound its spectral gap without appealing to isoperimetric inequalities and, for mixture targets, decompose the gap into a between-mode and a within-mode contribution, making precise the advantage of the diffusion path as an interpolation.
- •

In practice, the diffusion-path transition can only be run with approximate scores and a finite-time discretization. In Section3, we introduce the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm that removes the resulting score-approximation and discretization bias.
Because the proposal is constructed by a stochastic trajectory, the correction is carried out in an augmented path space. This contrasts with existing Metropolis adjustments for deterministic normalizing flows, where the flow is used either to generate independent proposals[24]or to reparametrize the target before applying local MCMC[34].
- •

Section4quantifies how the Metropolis acceptance probability depends on discretization and score approximation. With an approximate score, we show that the rejection probability is governed by the symmetrized Kullback–Leibler divergence between the approximate and exact reverse diffusion, or equivalently, an integrated squared score error.
- •

In Section5, we describe how the intermediate-time scores can be learned through path-space variational inference and discuss related work along this line. We also give practical guidelines for choosing the reference distribution, which we find crucial for mitigating mode collapse.
- •

Finally, in Section6, we evaluate the method on a range of Bayesian posteriors, study how diffusion time and step size affect sampling efficiency, and compare against tempering-based MCMC. Moreover, we show that scores learned by existing variational approaches can misrepresent the relative weights of isolated modes, or be inaccurate when the target’s score is highly nonlinear. In both cases, the Metropolis–Hastings correction is crucial for removing the resulting bias.

## 2Diffusion paths as global transitions

Bridging the target with a tractable reference through a sequence of intermediate distributions is a common strategy for constructing MCMC transitions that can move globally across separated modes. Here, we take the diffusion path as this bridge and argue that it is preferable to classical tempering.

Different choices of the reference process (14) recover or closely relate to several existing diffusion-based samplers. The path integral sampler[PIS,77]uses Brownian motion (ft=0,gt=1f_{t}=0,\,g_{t}=1) pinned at the origin, and the denoising diffusion sampler[DDS,71]uses an equilibrium OU process as the reference withpref=𝒩​(0,Id)p^{\mathrm{ref}}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}), for which the memoryless condition holds asT→∞T\to\infty; non-equilibrium annealed references are studied in[2,18]. A related line replaces the path-space variational objective with regression targets derived from the target score identity, enabling simulation-free or adjoint-based training[19,30,11], while others estimate the intermediate scores directly through Tweedie’s formula using importance sampling or MCMC[35,28].

A separate line of work directly learns the intermediate log-densities along the diffusion path[44,64,15,54], with which one can apply bias-correction methods such as annealed importance sampling or sequential Monte Carlo, as in[54]. However,Grenioux and Noble [29]show that such log-density estimates are often mode-blind: even when the underlying score is learned accurately, the estimated density can misrepresent the relative weight of separated modes, making the resulting bias correction unreliable. In contrast, MAD-Path depends only on the learned score, never on an intermediate log-density estimate. It forms a single-chain MCMC that is reversible with respect to the target and therefore corrects the modes’ relative weights even when the learned score gets them wrong. See Section6for a numerical illustration.

For multimodal targets, the reverse path-space KL objective is prone to mode collapse: it penalizes trajectories that place mass in low-density regions of the target far more heavily than those that miss well-separated modes altogether[72,31]. This mode-seeking behavior is characteristic of variational inference under reverse-KL objectives. A common remedy in multimodal MCMC is to add a mode-discovery component[55,68,67,40]. We follow the same principle in a two-stage strategy for learning the score: we first locate the modes, then build the reference process around them. Specifically, we takeprefp^{\mathrm{ref}}to be a Gaussian mixture whose components encode the located modes, which also keepsstrefs_{t}^{\mathrm{ref}}in closed form. Details are given in AppendixC.1; a similar strategy appears in[50].

We emphasize that MAD-Path is agnostic to how the score is obtained and how the reference is defined: the Metropolis–Hastings correction makesppthe exact invariant distribution for any approximate scores^t\widehat{s}_{t}. Score accuracy thus affects the acceptance probability, and hence efficiency, but never the distribution being sampled. Any of the approaches above, along with future improvements, may be used as applicable. For complex targets, however, constructing an informative score remains important in practice: incorporating known structure or problem-specific information about the target can be crucial for achieving high acceptance and efficient sampling.

## 6Numerical experiments

In this section, we present numerical experiments for our method (Algorithm1).
We evaluate its performance over six examples: a mixture of Gaussians with unequal variances; a mixture of well-separated skew-normal distributions; two Bayesian mixture models applied to real-world datasets: the Old Faithful geyser[7]and fetal deaths in litters[13]; a Bayesian model for sensor network localization[36]; and a seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) model[76]with multimodal profile likelihood.

On the first two synthetic targets, we compare MAD-Path against six baselines: adaptive parallel tempering (APT)[46], adaptive parallel tempering with deterministic even-odd swaps (APT-DEO)[52], tempered transition (TT)[47], annealed importance sampling (AIS)[48], sequential Monte Carlo (SMC)[27,20], as well as the unadjusted diffusion (UD) sampler, which uses the same learned scores^t\widehat{s}_{t}as MAD-Path but omits the Metropolis–Hastings correction.
All the tempering-based methods use ten MALA steps for each within-temperature transition.
We run APT and APT-DEO such that the swap rate, defined as the percentage of proposed state exchanges between parallel temperature chains that are successfully accepted, reaches around its theoretically optimal value of 0.234[6].
For TT, we calibrate the geometric inverse-temperature ladder and the per-level MALA step size to achieve a moderate acceptance rate.
For AIS and SMC, we use the adaptive schedule of[78].
In Section6.4, we examine the sensitivity of MAD-Path to the choice of horizonTTand number of stepsNN.
In the remaining examples, we mainly compare MAD-Path against APT-DEO, which proved the strongest competitor among the baselines.

We measure the computational cost of each method by the number of target score evaluations and ensure each method uses roughly the same number of evaluations.
MAD-Path is an amortized sampler: the target score is evaluated only during training, and the trained scores^t\widehat{s}_{t}, which does not involve the target score, is used during MCMC sampling. Therefore, the cost of gradient evaluation of MAD-Path only comes from the training phase, while the other MCMC methods (with MALA as the within-temperature transition) require target score evaluations at every step.
The total wall clock time, including both training and sampling, is also comparable, as listed in SectionC.2.

## 7Discussion

We introduced MAD-Path, a Metropolis–Hastings sampler that builds nonlocal proposals from a forward–backward diffusion path. The sampler leaves the target invariant even under an approximate score function and discretization error. We showed that the ideal diffusion-path transition has favorable mixing properties, and we characterized how the acceptance probability of MAD-Path degrades under score and discretization error. Finally, we discussed practical considerations and demonstrated the method on a range of challenging multimodal targets.

Several avenues remain for future work. On the methodological side, an important open problem is to develop score-learning methods better aligned with MAD-Path. The algorithm is valid for any finite horizonTT, and, as shown in Section2, promoting inter-mode mixing only requiresTTto be large enough for the modes to overlap at timeTT. Existing path-space variational objectives, by contrast, are typically derived in the infinite-TTlimit. Moreover, the acceptance probability of MAD-Path is governed by a symmetrized path-space KL divergence between the exact and approximate reverse diffusions (Theorem4.2), whereas common training objectives minimize a one-sided reverse KL (16). Designing finite-TTobjectives that better reflect the acceptance probability of MAD-Path might improve practical efficiency.

On the theoretical side, it would be interesting to establish the mixing time of MAD-Path. The present analysis separates the ideal diffusion-path transition from the effects of score and discretization error. A promising route is to view MAD-Path as a hybrid data-augmentation chain on path space. The comparison theory of Markov chains[5,56]may then yield spectral-gap bounds for MAD-Path by combining the gap of the ideal diffusion-path kernel (Section2) with a conditional mixing bound for the path-space Metropolis update.

The GMM parameters are estimated in two stages: first,NNparticles are evolved under an annealed Langevin algorithm with inverse-temperature schedule0<β1<⋯<βL=10<\beta_{1}<\cdots<\beta_{L}=1and adaptive step sizeshℓ=h/(βℓ+ε)h_{\ell}=h/(\beta_{\ell}+\varepsilon), to obtain samples covering all major modes.
