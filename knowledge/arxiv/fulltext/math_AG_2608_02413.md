## Abstract

We classify complements of cubic hypersurfaces in complex projective spaces of dimension at least two, including reducible and nonreduced cubics. The complement is Oka unless the cubic is the union of three distinct hyperplanes containing a common projective subspace of codimension two; this exceptional complement is not Oka. For a smooth cubic, we pass to an unramified cyclic triple cover of the complement and construct complement-preserving sprays along residual affine conics. On cubic surfaces the twenty-seven lines give a finite dominating family. In higher dimensions we rescale the sprays so that they descend from blowups along lines, and use the tangent-transfer geometry of Kaliman--Zaidenberg to make their tangent directions span at every point. For an irreducible singular cubic, projection from a singular point, Kusakabe's localization theorem, and Hanysz's theorem on meromorphic-graph complements yield an induction on dimension. Reducible cubics reduce to complements of affine hypersurfaces defined by polynomials of degree at most two; explicit complete vector fields and elementary normal forms isolate the exceptional configuration.

## Abstract.

We classify complements of cubic hypersurfaces in complex projective
spaces of dimension at least two, including reducible and nonreduced
cubics. The complement is Oka unless the cubic is the union of three
distinct hyperplanes containing a common projective subspace of
codimension two; this exceptional complement is not Oka. For a smooth cubic, we pass to
an unramified cyclic triple cover of the complement and construct
complement-preserving sprays along residual affine conics. On cubic
surfaces the twenty-seven lines give a finite dominating family. In
higher dimensions we rescale the sprays so that they descend from
blowups along lines, and use the tangent-transfer geometry of
Kaliman–Zaidenberg to make their tangent directions span at every
point. For an irreducible singular cubic, projection from a singular
point, Kusakabe’s localization theorem, and Hanysz’s theorem on
meromorphic-graph complements yield an induction on dimension.
Reducible cubics reduce to complements of affine hypersurfaces defined
by polynomials of degree at most two; explicit complete vector fields
and elementary normal forms isolate the exceptional configuration.

## 1.Introduction

Oka theory originates in Oka’s 1939 solution of the second Cousin
problem on domains of holomorphy[39]. Grauert extended the
principle to principal and associated holomorphic bundles with complex
Lie-group or homogeneous fibres over Stein spaces[21,22,23]. Gromov then
placed the subject in the framework of thehh-principle and
introduced elliptic manifolds through dominating holomorphic sprays[25, §§0.5–0.6]. The spray method for bundles and
submersions was developed further by Forstnerič–Prezelj[9,10]. Forstnerič
introduced subellipticity and proved its Oka principle[12]; he later identified convex approximation as a
central analytic Oka property[13,14].
We refer to[15,17,20]and[18]for broader
accounts.

Producing concrete new Oka manifolds is itself a persistent difficulty:
the flexibility encoded by the definition does not usually make a
given manifold recognizable. Several complementary mechanisms have
nevertheless enlarged the class. Forstnerič proved that manifolds
of class𝒜\mathscr{A}are algebraically subelliptic[11, Corollary 2.4], while smooth flexible
affine varieties admit dominating sprays[3, Proposition 6.3]. Kaliman and Kutzschebauch
established the algebraic density property for the suspension hypersurfacesu​v=p​(x¯)uv=p(\bar{x})when the zero fibre ofppis smooth and reduced[33]; the generalized density
criterion of Andrist–Freudenburg–Huang–Kutzschebauch–Schott yields
further Stein examples with the density property[2, Corollary 4.7 and Theorem 4.8]. Since a Stein
manifold with the density property is elliptic, these examples are Oka[18, Proposition 5.6.23].

Blowups provide another source. Lárusson and Truong proved algebraic
subellipticity for blowups of manifolds of class𝒜\mathscr{A}along
algebraic submanifolds[38], and
Kaliman–Kutzschebauch–Truong obtained
subellipticity for blowups of locally stably flexible manifolds along
smooth centres[34]. Other constructions
show that Oka flexibility reaches beyond weak subellipticity: Kusakabe
constructed Oka complements that need not be weakly subelliptic[35,37], and
Forstnerič–Kusakabe found Oka tube domains in holomorphic line
bundles[16].

Against this background, hypersurface complements form a particularly
sharp test of the boundary
between Oka flexibility and Kobayashi hyperbolicity. Even among plane
cubics, the known results left one natural case unresolved. Hanysz
classified Oka complements of
projective hyperplane arrangements and treated certain
meromorphic-graph complements[27, Theorems 3.1 and 4.6].
Hanysz also proved that the complements of both smooth affine-conic
types—equivalently, the tangent and transverse line–conic
configurations—are Oka[28, Appendix B]. Kusakabe
proved that the complement
of every irreducible singular plane cubic is Oka[36, Corollary 4.9(2), p. 1050]. Related
complement results appear in[37,19]. The smooth plane cubic,
however, remained a long-standing open case: the question was posed in[15, Problem B, p. 33], revisited in[17, §2.3.4, pp. 758–759], and still recorded in[20, Remark 5.6, p. 394]. Buzzard and Lu proved
dominability byℂ2\mathbb{C}^{2}[7, Proposition 5.1], but their
map is ramified, and the Oka property is not known to descend through
arbitrary finite ramified maps. Oka-1 flexibility[1]does not by itself give the full Oka property
either.

The residual-conic method has an important compact antecedent. Smooth
cubic surfaces are uniformly rational[5, Example 2.4],
and complete uniformly rational varieties are algebraically elliptic[4, Theorem 1.3]. More generally,
Kaliman and Zaidenberg proved that every smooth cubic hypersurface of
dimension at least two is algebraically elliptic[32, Theorem 1.1]. In dimensions at least three,
their proof supplies the two geometric inputs used here: lines through
a general point span the tangent space[32, Lemma 3.2], and the third-point involution
transports these directions to residual conics through an arbitrary
point[32, Proposition 3.7, Remark 3.8, and the proof of
Theorem 1.1]. Their use of this involution follows
Bogomolov–Böhning and Bogomolov–Karzhemanov–Kuyumzhiyan[5,6].

The compact result alone does not control a prescribed complement.
Our construction turns the residual-conic directions into
complement-preserving entire sprays. For surfaces, the conic bundles
and the2727-line configuration are classical[8, Chapter 9]; for a modern account of the geometry of
lines on cubic hypersurfaces, see[29]. The global
Hamiltonian time line and its use on the complement are analytic
ingredients specific to the present
proof. The higher-dimensional argument is inspired by this surface
picture but must overcome two additional obstacles: blowing up along a
line is no longer the identity, and no fixed2727-line configuration
is available. A canonical rescaling makes the flow descend through
the exceptional fibres, while the imported Kaliman–Zaidenberg
geometry supplies domination. Proper-fibre descent and a Noetherian
finite extraction complete the argument.

The cyclic-covering device itself is standard in logarithmic geometry.
It replaces a logarithmic pair by a hypersurface with one additional
coordinate; see, for example,[40, §3.1]. What is
specific here is its combination with the complement-preserving
residual-conic sprays.
