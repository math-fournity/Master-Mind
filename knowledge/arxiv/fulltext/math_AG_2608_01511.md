## Abstract

We prove that a general Brill--Noether curve $C$ of genus $g \geq 2$ and degree $d$ in $\mathbb{P}^4$ has stable normal bundle $N_C$ if and only if $$(g, d) \notin \{(2,6), (3,7), (5,8), (6,9), (7,10)\}.$$ Moreover, $N_C$ is strictly semistable if $(g, d) \in \{(3, 7), (5, 8)\}$, and is unstable if $(g, d) \in \{(2, 6), (6, 9), (7, 10)\}$. Our results are valid in any characteristic. Along the way, we also generalize previous results of Larson--Vogt on interpolation for $N_C(-1)$, from characteristic zero to arbitrary characteristic.

## 1.Introduction

Letρ​(g,r,d)=g−(r+1)​(g−d+r)\rho(g,r,d)=g-(r+1)(g-d+r)be the Brill–Noether number. By the Brill–Noether Theorem, a general curve of genusggadmits a nondegenerate map toℙr\mathbb{P}^{r}of degreeddif and only ifρ​(g,r,d)≥0\rho(g,r,d)\geq 0[GH80]. Moreover, in this case there is a unique component of the Hilbert scheme whose general point parameterizes nondegenerate (smooth ifr≥3r\geq 3) curves of genusggand degreedd, and whose moduli map dominates the moduli space of genusggcurvesMgM_{g}[FL81,G82,EH87](for proofs in arbitrary characteristic see[W85,JP14,LLV25]). A curve in this component is called aBrill–Noether curveorBN-curvefor short.
In this paper, we characterize the pairs(g,d)(g,d)for which the general BN-curve of genusggand degreeddinℙ4\mathbb{P}^{4}has (semi)stable normal bundle. We work over an algebraically closed field of arbitrary characteristic.

The normal bundleNC=NC/ℙrN_{C}=N_{C/\mathbb{P}^{r}}of a curveCCinℙr\mathbb{P}^{r}controls the deformations ofCCinℙr\mathbb{P}^{r}and plays a crucial role in applications to arithmetic and moduli. Stability is among the most important properties of a vector bundle. To define it, recall that
the slopeμ\muof a vector bundleEEon a curveCCis defined byμ​(E)=deg⁡(E)rk⁡(E).\mu(E)=\frac{\deg(E)}{\operatorname{rk}(E)}.The bundleEEis(semi)stableif every proper subbundleFFofEEsatisfiesμ​(F)​<(−)​μ​(E).\mu(F)\underset{{\scriptscriptstyle(}-{\scriptscriptstyle)}}{<}\mu(E).

(Semi)stable vector bundles are the building blocks of all vector bundles under filtration, much in the same way that prime (powers) are the building blocks of all integers under multiplication. That is, every vector bundle on a curve admits a unique filtration, called theHarder–Narasimhan (HN) filtration, with semistable quotients of decreasing slope,
and every semistable vector bundle admits a filtration, called aJordan–Hölder (JH) filtration, with stable quotients of the same slope, which are unique up to permutation.

Consequently, the stability of normal bundles is a central question in geometry. This question was completely settled for general BN-curves inℙ3\mathbb{P}^{3}by[CLV22]. In higher dimensions, the stability of normal bundles is only known in some cases[BR99,CS25,CLV26,R23]. We refer the reader to[CS25, §1.3]for a more detailed history of the problem and further references. Our main theorem completely settles this fundamental question for general BN-curves inℙ4\mathbb{P}^{4}.

Our second theorem concerns when curves can pass through certain configurations of points.
The study of such questions goes all the way back to Euclid, who showed that circles can pass through three given points in the plane — provided that those points are not collinear.

In the context of modern Brill–Noether theory, Larson–Vogt[LV22]determined the number of general points a BN-curve can pass through.
It is natural to further ask when a BN-curve of genusggand degreeddcan pass through points as some subset of the points specialize onto a hyperplane. By Bézout’s theorem, we cannot specialize more thanddpoints onto a hyperplane. Since the normal bundle of a curve controls its deformation theory, this question is intimately linked to a cohomological property known as interpolation for the twistNC​(−1)N_{C}(-1).

This question was completely settled for BN-curves inℙ3\mathbb{P}^{3}in characteristic zero by[V18,L21], and in positive characteristic by[L24]. Inℙ4\mathbb{P}^{4}, an answer to this question was previously only known in characteristic zero[LV21], and in even higher dimensions only partial results are known[B20].
Along the way to proving our main theorem, we also completely settle this question for BN-curves inℙ4\mathbb{P}^{4}in arbitrary characteristic.

## Overview of the proof

Section2reviews some preliminaries on the stability of vector bundles on nodal curves and modifications of vector bundles. Section3reduces the proofs of our theorems to a finite number of cases using a degeneration originally explored in[LV21]. Section4outlines the minor modifications to the argument of[LV21]that are needed to prove interpolation for the twisted normal bundle in arbitrary characteristic.
All told, this suffices to reduce the proof of Theorem1.1to2424low-degree cases.
As in most previous work on the stability of normal bundles, these low-degree cases are by far the most interesting and subtle ones — the low-degree cases are, after all, where the exceptions lie — and prior techniques are inadequate to handle them.
For this, we introduce two fundamental innovations, both of which hold promise for generalizing to higher dimensional projective spaces.

The first, in Section5, is a detailed analysis of the behavior of (semi)stability under a degeneration to the union of a curveCCwith a bisecant lineLL. Strikingly, we are able to deduce (semi)stability for the normal bundle of a smoothing ofC∪LC\cup Lby showing that several different modifications ofNCN_{C}are close to (semi)stable. This is a crucial difference; the bounds we need to prove for such modifications ofNCN_{C}are weaker than the bounds required for (semi)stability. Applying this construction iteratively thereby reduces the problem of (semi)stability to verifying bounds on the slopes of subbundles which get progressively weaker as the degree gets larger.

The second, in Section6, is a method for showing that making such modifications to bundles on elliptic curves which are “sufficiently unstable” produces bundles which are “less unstable.” This allows us to efficiently verify the desired slope bounds after pulling off bisecant lines to obtain elliptic curves.

Finally, in Section7, we apply these two tools to verify the majority of the2424low-degree cases, and handle the remaining ones by ad hoc methods.
