## Abstract

The boundaries of cooperative helix--coil transitions directly affect protein allostery and conformational dynamics, yet the physical origin of the persistent one-to-two-residue assignment ambiguity at these structural interfaces remains unresolved. We apply the discrete Hasimoto map to translate three-dimensional protein backbone geometry into a one-dimensional discrete nonlinear Schrödinger effective potential and analyze its spatial-frequency fluctuations. Helical segments display near-integrable, low-entropy soliton-like states, while coil regions exhibit broadband conformational noise. Statistical analysis of over 19,000 boundaries across 1,986 proteins reveals a median geometric transition width of only 0.145 residues, providing an independent kinematic counterpart to the high thermodynamic cooperativity of the Zimm--Bragg model. This sub-residue spatial narrowness indicates an intrinsic observational constraint governed by the Gabor uncertainty principle, whereby any macroscopic spectral probe tends to blur the microscopic phase boundary, suggesting that the boundary ambiguity in structural biology is not merely algorithmic but reflects a physical resolution limit inherent to the biopolymer lattice.

The protein backbone constitutes a semiflexible heteropolymer chain[64,35]whose secondary structures (α\alpha-helices,β\beta-sheets, and coils) arise from a competition between hydrogen-bond enthalpies and conformational entropy. This competition operates at the scale of a fewkB​Tk_{B}T, and the helix–coil transition represents a canonical one-dimensional highly cooperative order–disorder transition[68], often described as a pseudo-phase transition in finite-size biopolymers owing to the absence of true long-range order in one-dimensional systems with short-range interactions. Recent topological analysis of configuration-space isopotential hypersurfaces supports its thermodynamic phase-transition character in the topological sense[16]. Precisely delineating the geometric boundaries of these structural phases is relevant to macromolecular folding[26], allosteric signal propagation, and conformational dynamics, since allosteric transitions often involve one-to-two-residue displacements of helical boundaries and thermal helix fraying induces analogous sub-residue fluctuations at sub-nanosecond timescales. At each Cαpositionnnalong the backbone, the bond-angle curvatureκ​[n]\kappa[n]and dihedral torsionτ​[n]\tau[n]characterize the three-dimensional space curve; inα\alpha-helical segments both quantities adopt nearly constant values dictated by regular hydrogen-bonding patterns, while in coil regions they fluctuate widely. Given these intrinsic conformational fluctuations, a persistent observation is that secondary-structure assignment algorithms, whether based on hydrogen-bond energetics[30,22], backbone geometry[38,44,32], sequence-based prediction[52], or end-to-end deep learning[28], systematically disagree by one to two residues at helix–coil interfaces. Whether this persistent ambiguity reflects differing algorithmic definitions or an intrinsic spatial resolution limit imposed by the backbone geometry itself remains unresolved.

Differential-geometric and spectral approaches have probed backbone structure from complementary angles. Curvature–torsion analyses of protein space curves[51,42,19]and signal-processing methods that extract periodicities from one-dimensional physicochemical profiles[17,18,10,7,48,4,56,55,11,59]have each illuminated aspects of secondary-structure regularity, yet these methods predominantly operate on scalar sequences rather than the full three-dimensional backbone geometry, and none provides a physical mechanism governing boundary sharpness.

A natural framework for connecting three-dimensional backbone geometry to one-dimensional physical fields originates in vortex filament dynamics. The Hasimoto map[24]combines curvature and torsion into a complex scalar field governed by the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, whose soliton solutions motivated the topological-soliton description of protein folds[8,14]. The discrete formulation of this map[13,25]yields a discrete nonlinear Schrödinger equation (DNLS), under whichα\alpha-helices emerge as soliton excitations with sub-angstrom parametric accuracy across thousands of proteins[45,36]. Recent extensions have modeled topological transitions via Arnold perestroikas[2]and connected soliton dynamics to protein knot formation[3], while independent work has identified secondary structures through discrete Frenet criteria[49]. These studies establish the descriptive power of the DNLS soliton picture but treat known structures as fitting targets (the inverse problem) without exploiting the spectral content of the effective potential for forward physical analysis. Recent work[61]derived an exact closed-form decomposition of the DNLS effective potential and demonstrated that the Hasimoto map constitutes a kinematic identity rather than a dynamical equation, identifying three structural barriers (chirality encoding, local-geometry dominance, and the failure of self-consistent iteration) that prevent global predictive use. Subsequent research[60]introduced the concept of piecewise integrability, showing that helical segments correspond to near-integrable islands where the analytic dispersion relation reconstructs backbone coordinates with sub-angstrom accuracy (median RMSD=0.77=0.77Å), and that the integrability residualE​[n]E[n]maps the geometric boundaries of these islands. These results raise the question of whether the intrinsic physical width of the helix–coil phase boundary, as encoded in the spectral structure of the effective potential, imposes a fundamental limit on structural resolution.

In the spatial-frequency domain, protein secondary structures exhibit a clear order–disorder separation. Helical segments appear as near-integrable, low-entropy soliton-like states whose spectral power is concentrated at the zero-frequency mode, reflecting their geometrically uniform character. Coil regions, by contrast, exhibit broadband conformational fluctuations distributed across all spatial frequencies, consistent with structural disorder. The phase boundaries separating these two regimes are remarkably sharp, with sub-residue geometric transition widths and a directional asymmetry in which helix exits are sharper than helix entries.

This sub-residue spatial narrowness reveals an intrinsic observational constraint governed by the Gabor uncertainty principle[23]. Expanding the spectral observation window improves frequency resolution but progressively blurs the sub-residue phase boundary. This trade-off imposes a maximal-localization constraint under which phase-boundary distinguishability is maximized at the narrowest feasible window, providing a physical rationale for the observation that the pointwise integrability residualE​[n]E[n]achieves the highest boundary discriminability among all structural descriptors tested[61,60].

Guided by this physical duality, we construct a multi-scale structural characterization framework (Fig.1). The discrete Hasimoto map translates three-dimensional backbone geometry into a one-dimensional effective potential (Fig.1a), mapping structurally ordered helices to near-integrable soliton-like states and disordered coils to broadband conformational fluctuations (Fig.1b). The pointwise integrability residualE​[n]E[n]serves as a local order parameter that captures torsion discontinuities at structural phase boundaries, while a complementary low-frequency energy ratioRLFR_{\mathrm{LF}}serves as a macroscopic spectral probe that measures the spectral uniformity of helical interiors (Fig.1c). Combining these multi-scale observables reconciles local boundary precision with global topological robustness under the Gabor uncertainty constraint and improves structural-state separability. This framework suggests that broadband, high-entropy geometries may correspond to the conformational flexibility associated with allosteric transitions and protein interactions, a hypothesis that motivates future validation against experimental dynamics data.

The protein backbone can be viewed as a one-dimensional lattice indexed by residue numbernn, and the effective potentialVre​[n]V_{\mathrm{re}}[n]as a scalar field defined on this lattice. In ordered helical segments,Vre​[n]V_{\mathrm{re}}[n]is approximately translationally invariant (constant alongnn), reflecting the spatial periodicity imposed by regular hydrogen-bonding geometry. The helix–coil transition breaks this local translational symmetry: coil regions exhibit rapid, aperiodic fluctuations inVreV_{\mathrm{re}}. The short-time Fourier transform (STFT) provides a natural probe of this local symmetry breaking by decomposingVre​[n]V_{\mathrm{re}}[n]into spatial-frequency components within a sliding window. Because the independent variable is the residue index rather than time, the resulting transform yields spatial frequencies that measure structural periodicity along the backbone. The zero-frequency (DC) component corresponds to a segment of constantVreV_{\mathrm{re}}, implying uniform curvatureκ\kappaand torsionτ\tau; in three dimensions, such a segment traces a regular helix of fixed radius and pitch. Near-integrable helical segments concentrate spectral power at this DC mode, while coil regions distribute power broadly across all spatial frequencies. The local power spectrum thus provides a natural basis for quantifying the degree of local structural order.

## 4 Discussion & Conclusion

The spectral contrast between DC-dominated helical segments and broadband coil regions, established quantitatively in Sec.3, provides a frequency-domain rationale for the empirical success of the pointwise integrability residualE​[n]E[n][61,60]. BecauseE​[n]E[n]measures the local violation of the DNLS dispersion relation, it vanishes within the near-constant helical plateau and responds sharply to boundary discontinuities where torsion non-uniformity disrupts integrability. Combined with the sub-residue sharpness of helix–coil transitions, this contrast renders any finite-width spectral window susceptible to boundary contamination, giving rise to an intrinsic spatial-spectral trade-off between boundary precision and robustness to local conformational defects.

This intrinsic trade-off implies that relying solely on the pointwise local limit is topologically fragile[20]. Isolated torsion defects within an otherwise continuous helix trigger fragmentation of the macroscopic domain. Conversely, widening the observation window to absorb such local noise necessarily blurs the sub-residue phase boundaries. An ideal discrete nonlinear Schrödinger (DNLS) soliton propagates without dispersing its spectral content, producing minimal spectral entropy; real helices approximate this integrable limit. The local order parameter (E​[n]E[n]) detects the rupture of this near-integrability at boundaries and defects, while the macroscopic spectral probe (RLFR_{\mathrm{LF}}) measures the preservation of the DC-dominated spectral core within helical interiors. Their combination captures complementary structural information not fully accessible to either observable alone, raising the phase-state separability from AUC 0.783 to 0.815.

The sub-residue sharpness of helix–coil transitions (median widthw=0.145w=0.145residues across 19,148 fitted boundaries) requires careful physical interpretation. On the discrete peptide lattice, this value means that the sigmoid transition in spectral entropy is effectively complete between oneCαC_{\alpha}position and the next, with no intermediate so-called half-helical state; the fitted continuous width should therefore be understood as a measure of the abruptness of the underlying discrete jump rather than as a literal sub-atomic spatial extent. This step-like character finds a natural thermodynamic counterpart in the Zimm–Bragg helix–coil model[68,21], a foundational framework in polymer and soft matter physics, where the small nucleation parameter (σZB≪1\sigma_{\mathrm{ZB}}\ll 1) compresses the coexistence region to one or two residues. The sub-residue geometric sharpness observed here provides an independent, purely kinematic observation consistent with this high degree of thermodynamic cooperativity. While the Hasimoto effective potential is a purely kinematic construct derived from static coordinates, this sub-residue sharpness serves as a structural footprint of the underlying energetic cooperativity that governs helix nucleation and propagation. The observed sharpness is also consistent with the backbone conformational entropy changeΔ​SBB=5.2±0.3\Delta S_{\mathrm{BB}}=5.2\pm 0.3cal mol-1K-1per residue recently measured by calorimetry for the helix-to-coil transition[67]. The directional asymmetry quantified in Sec.3(helix exits being sharper than helix entries) is likewise consistent with the cooperative thermodynamics of the Zimm–Bragg framework, in which the free-energy landscape for helix propagation differs from that for nucleation[41], providing a macroscopic energetic context for the local hydrogen-bond kinematic mechanism identified therein.

This geometric abruptness has a direct observational consequence through the Gabor limit[23]. Widening the STFT observation window improves spectral resolution but progressively blurs the sub-residue boundary. Because the geometric transition approximates a sub-residue step function, any window expansion beyond the minimal scale introduces boundary contamination, and the monotonic decay of AUC with increasingσ\sigmais an empirical manifestation of this trade-off. The failure of the adaptive per-chain routing strategy to surpass the pointwise baseline further illustrates that the conflict between boundary precision and robustness to local conformational defects operates at the residue level, not the chain level. This trade-off is a general constraint of windowed spectral analysis applied to structural boundary detection, not a limitation specific to the present method. It provides a physical explanation for the well-known observation that existing secondary structure assignment algorithms, including DSSP, STRIDE, and their successors, systematically disagree at helix–coil boundaries by one to two residues[44,31]. The inter-algorithm discrepancy is not merely a consequence of differing definitions or energy-cutoff choices, but reflects an intrinsic spatial-spectral uncertainty that no finite-resolution geometric probe can circumvent. The present framework is therefore not intended to supersede these established assignment tools in speed or coverage, but to identify the physical origin of their boundary behavior and to quantify the resolution limit that constrains any geometry-based structural probe. The AUC values reported here accordingly quantify the intrinsic geometric distinguishability of the backbone under this constraint. Residue-level multi-resolution fusion or nonlinear probe combinations may partially alleviate it and remain directions for future work.

A primary methodological limitation is the reliance on X-ray crystallographic structures, which represent low-temperature or cryogenic single-conformer snapshots. Under physiological conditions, thermal fluctuations induce helix-end fraying and breathing motions[68]that would broaden the helix–coil interface; the median width of0.1450.145residues should therefore be regarded as a lower bound characteristic of the crystallographic ensemble rather than a general constant. The high-frequency geometric noise that triggers domain fragmentation byE​[n]E[n]in the case studies (Sec.3) represents genuine local conformational features, including proline-induced kinks, coiled-coil superhelical distortions, and backbone flexibility frozen in the crystal lattice, rather than experimental coordinate errors, which at 2.0 Å resolution perturbκ\kappaby only∼0.01\sim 0.01rad, far below the∼0.5\sim 0.5rad torsion jumps that activateE​[n]E[n]. The STFT smoothing thus performs a physically meaningful operation, averaging over local conformational heterogeneity to recover the macroscopic helical topology. The Hasimoto map encodes exclusively theCαC_{\alpha}backbone kinematics (bond-angle curvature and dihedral torsion) and is by construction blind to side-chain packing, hydrophobic interactions, and solvent effects, all of which contribute substantially to folding thermodynamics. The spectral signatures reported here thus characterize the backbone geometric component of secondary structure and do not capture the full energetic landscape. The standardized linear combination used to fuse the local and macroscopic observables is the simplest possible combination strategy; nonlinear methods or locally adaptive weighting schemes may yield further improvements, though at the cost of additional free parameters. The discrete Frenet frame uses forward differencesΔ​𝐫​[n]=𝐫​[n+1]−𝐫​[n]\Delta\mathbf{r}[n]=\mathbf{r}[n{+}1]-\mathbf{r}[n]following Ref.[13]. Repeating the full pipeline with centered differences(𝐫​[n+1]−𝐫​[n−1])/2(\mathbf{r}[n{+}1]-\mathbf{r}[n{-}1])/2preserves all qualitative conclusions, with the spectral entropy ordering⟨Hspec⟩helix<⟨Hspec⟩coil\langle H_{\mathrm{spec}}\rangle_{\mathrm{helix}}<\langle H_{\mathrm{spec}}\rangle_{\mathrm{coil}}, the integrability residual ordering⟨E⟩helix<⟨E⟩coil\langle E\rangle_{\mathrm{helix}}<\langle E\rangle_{\mathrm{coil}}, and the mean helicalEEvalue (0.1320.132vs.0.1330.133) essentially unchanged; the centered-difference AUC values are numerically higher because the smoothed tangent amplifies the spectral contrast between near-integrable helices and disordered coils, confirming that the forward-difference choice is conservative and the conclusions are not artifacts of the discretization (TableS1). The boundary analysis relies on DSSP[30]assignments as reference annotations, and DSSP itself employs a fixed hydrogen-bond energy threshold that introduces its own boundary ambiguity. Alternative assignment algorithms (e.g., STRIDE[22]) or torsion-angle clustering approaches[31]may shift individual boundary positions by one to two residues, although the statistical conclusions drawn from>>19,000 boundaries are unlikely to be qualitatively affected. The multi-scale framework also has an intrinsic minimum chain-length requirement imposed by the STFT window. Atσ=1.5\sigma=1.5, the Gaussian window spansM=9M=9residues; combined with the Frenet-frame end trimming (±2\pm 2residues), a chain ofNNCαatoms yields onlyN−12N-12validHspecH_{\mathrm{spec}}values. Chains shorter than approximately 20 residues therefore provide too few spectral estimates for the windowed probe to characterize helical interiors reliably, and for chains below 13 residues noHspecH_{\mathrm{spec}}value can be computed at all. In this short-peptide regime, relevant to de novo peptide design and antimicrobial peptide engineering, the pointwise integrability residualE​[n]E[n], which requires onlyN≥7N\geq 7and carries no window constraint, remains the sole applicable geometric descriptor. For intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs), the framework correctly identifies the entire chain as broadband and high-entropy, but the absence of DC-dominated helical segments means that the macroscopic probeRLFR_{\mathrm{LF}}provides no complementary information, reducing the multi-scale framework to the single-observableE​[n]E[n]limit.

The static-snapshot limitation points directly to a natural extension of this work. Because the Hasimoto map is defined at each instantaneous backbone configuration, it can be applied frame-by-frame to molecular dynamics (MD) trajectories, yielding a time-dependent effective potentialVre​[n,t]V_{\mathrm{re}}[n,t]. A joint spatial-temporal spectral decomposition of this field would enable tracking of helix-end breathing, the propagation of conformational perturbations along the backbone, and the spectral signatures of folding or allosteric transitions during their temporal evolution, building on systematic characterizations of protein family conformational diversity[40]. More broadly, the spectral entropyHspecH_{\mathrm{spec}}provides a sequence-agnostic, purely geometric measure of local conformational variability on the 1D backbone lattice, complementing the graph-derived local Shannon entropy recently introduced for conformational substates in molecular dynamics[54]and directly connecting the geometric order parameter to the soft-matter concept of conformational entropy density. High-entropy broadband regions naturally correspond to conformationally flexible segments such as hinges, loops, and intrinsically disordered regions[65]that have been associated with allosteric communication and protein–protein interactions[62]. For IDPs in particular, the static broadband signature correctly identifies the absence of stable helical order but cannot resolve transient local structuring events such as helical nucleation, nascentβ\beta-hairpin formation, and the emergence of cryptic binding pockets[46,50]that govern molecular recognition and function in these entropy-dominated systems. The time-resolved extension to MD trajectories is therefore especially relevant for detecting when and where fleeting geometric order nucleates within the disordered ensemble. This question carries direct therapeutic significance, given that transient pre-structured conformations in IDPs have recently been shown to serve as druggable targets through conformational selection[6]. Unlike sequence-based spectral methods that transform one-dimensional physicochemical indices into frequency representations[56,11,59], the present framework operates directly on the three-dimensional differential geometry of the folded backbone, offering a complementary structural perspective. Whether this geometric spectral fingerprint can quantitatively predict functional flexibility or allosteric pathways, potentially serving as an input feature for flexibility predictors[34], remains to be validated against experimental dynamics data and larger-scale functional annotations. A further limitation of the present Cα-only Hasimoto map is that it is by construction blind to the peptide-plane degrees of freedom, specifically the N–Cα–C′=O dihedral constraints and amide hydrogen positions, that directly mediate backbone hydrogen bonding. Extending the map to incorporate this full peptide-plane geometry would provide access to the vibrational coordinates that underpin the Davydov soliton model[14]of nonlinear energy transfer alongα\alpha-helices, potentially bridging the present kinematic description with a Hamiltonian treatment of backbone dynamics that explicitly accounts for the N–H⋯\cdotsO=C hydrogen-bond network. More generally, because the Hasimoto map operates on any discrete space curve regardless of chemical identity, the spectral-entropy framework is in principle applicable to any soft matter system whose backbone geometry undergoes cooperative order–disorder transitions. Natural candidates include DNA supercoiling, where plectoneme nucleation represents an analogous symmetry-breaking event on a semiflexible polymer[12,29], and the helix–coil equilibria of synthetic polypeptoids[33,53], where the absence of backbone hydrogen-bond donors produces a distinct cooperativity landscape. The geometric-spectral mapping developed here thus offers a unified framework for characterizing structural phase boundaries across the broader class of semiflexible biopolymers and synthetic macromolecules that constitute canonical soft condensed matter systems.

In summary, this study establishes the helix–coil boundary of protein backbones as a model system for investigating observational limits in soft matter structural characterization. The sub-residue sharpness of helix–coil transitions (medianw=0.145w=0.145residues) provides independent kinematic evidence for the high thermodynamic cooperativity described by the Zimm–Bragg model[68], and the resulting Gabor uncertainty constraint explains both the strengths and the vulnerabilities of geometry-based structural probes at different spatial scales. The multi-scale characterization framework, combining a local order parameter with a macroscopic spectral probe, offers a principled response to this physical constraint and improves structural-state separability beyond either observable alone. The central contribution is the identification of a fundamental observational limit, namely that the Gabor spatial-spectral uncertainty principle governs any spectral approach to detecting structural phase boundaries on a discrete polymer lattice, providing a physical explanation for the well-known inter-algorithm boundary discrepancies in structural biology and offering a unified geometric perspective applicable to cooperative transitions in biopolymers and synthetic soft matter systems.
