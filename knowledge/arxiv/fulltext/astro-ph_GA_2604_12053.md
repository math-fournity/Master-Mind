## Abstract

Self-gravitating systems of $N$ particles are chaotic. We wonder how chaotic the Galaxy is, and what the consequences are. We therefore simulate the dynamical evolution of a galaxy-scale distribution of point masses in order to measure the degree of chaos in such a system. These calculations were performed using the softened gravitational $N$-body tree-code Bonsai, with up to 40 million equal-mass particles. Smaller simulations were performed to establish the scaling of the Lyapunov time $t_L$ with $N$. We establish the relations between the degree of chaos, the number of particles, and the softening length in the gravitational force calculation of large-scale $N$-body simulations. The moment the bar forms appears insensitive to infinitesimal perturbations to the initial realisation. In contrast, the bar strength and its further evolution sensitively depend on such perturbations. Interestingly enough, the run-to-run variation in the bar strength has its maximum around the maximum bar strength, and drops to the moment the bar buckles. The galaxies we simulated are highly chaotic, but the softening in the simulations suppresses chaos. Still, our models show considerable variations in the macroscopic behaviour due to infinitesimal perturbations to the initial conditions. Real galaxies, however, should be orders of magnitude more chaotic than our simulations, and we are unable to quantify their consequences. Smooth galactic potentials to study individual stellar orbits should be handled with caution on timescales longer than the Lyapunov time. Extrapolating to the number of stars in the Galaxy, ignoring planets and other minor bodies, we conclude that the Milky Way-size galaxies are chaotic on a timescale $\lesssim 0.1$ Myr.

## 1Introduction

The self-gravitatingNN-body problem forN>2N>2is chaotic(Poincaré,1891). Any system of galaxies,
stars, planets, or minor bodies exhibits chaotic behaviour. As a
consequence, infinitesimal perturbations to the system grow
exponentially until they reach a magnitude comparable to the size of
the system(Dejonghe & Hut,1986). By that time, the system under
study forms only one possible realisation of the starting
conditions. We classically measure this growth in phase space by a
magnification factor of the perturbation over a certain time
interval. The reciprocal of this measure is sometimes referred to as
the Lyapunov time(Lyapunov,1892). We naively adopt this metric
on a time interval within which we require an infinitesimal
perturbation to grow over a pre-determined number of magnitudes. A
chaotic system then leads to unpredictable behaviour over several
Lyapunov timescales.

The median Lyapunov timescale for three equal-mass particles
corresponds roughly to the crossing time(Boekholt & Portegies Zwart,2025). Such crossing timescale scaling
continues up to aboutN∼32N\sim 32, when the system’s crossing
timescale and relaxation timescale become comparable(Goodman et al.,1993). For largerNN, and up toN∼217N\sim 2^{17},
the Lyapunov timescales are inversely proportional to the system’s
relaxation timescale(Hemsendorf & Merritt,2002). It is unclear what
happens for even larger values ofNN, for example, on galactic scales of size and structure.
We call this the paradox of infinite granularity(Portegies Zwart & Boekholt,2023).

Sellwood & Debattista (2009)investigated stochasticity inNN-body
simulations of disc galaxies and found that even round-off level
differences in initial conditions can cause the macroscopic evolution
of two models to diverge. They demonstrated thatNN-body galaxies are
chaotic, independent of the simulation code, numerical parameters, or
floating-point precision. Similar chaotic behaviours were observed
even on larger scales, in cosmological simulations.(e.g. Genel et al.,2019; Keller et al.,2019).

In this paper, we focus on dry galaxy models, composed ofNNequal-mass
particles distributed in a disc-halo structure. In the experiment, we
run each simulation twice using precisely the same initial
realisation. This would produce two indistinguishable results with zero phase-space distance.
However, in one of these runs, we introduce an infinitesimal perturbation to one of the
particles before performing the simulation. We subsequently study the
consequence of this introduced perturbation.

We measure the phase-space distance (in position and velocity) between
these two calculations. The growth of this distance measure is
exponential, consistent with a chaotic system; it depends on the
number of particles and on the softening of the gravitational force.
We also measure how the global structure of the perturbed
galaxy deviates from the non-perturbed galaxy. We use the
characteristics of the bar that naturally forms in such a galaxy
model to quantify the structural variation.

## 2.2Experiments

To investigate the chaotic behaviour of theNN-body system,
we compared the results of a series of paired simulations
which started from nearly identical initial conditions. In the first
simulation, referred to as the reference run orr_xx_0,
the initial conditions were sampled directly from the DF ofAgama.
Our code gives perfectly reproducible results, and if we
run this initial realisation twice, we obtain, to machine precision,
twice the same results. It could be considered a waste of computer
time to test this for each run, and therefore, we only tested this
bit-wise reproducibility for one realisation of the initial
conditions.

For the second simulation, referred to as the perturbed run orr_xx_1, we introduced an infinitesimal perturbation to the
initial realisation of the reference run. We generated a unique
realisation by randomly selecting one particle in the reference run
and displacing it radially byδ​R=50\delta R=50pc. This leads to a relative initial
phase-space distance on the order of10−1010^{-10}in the dimensionlessNN-body unit(Hénon,1971). The precise value ofδ​R\delta Rhas no influence on our measured degree of chaos.111We confirmed that the measured Lyapunov time (see Section3.1.2for the definition)
is insensitive to the value ofδ​R\delta Rby varying it from 25 pc to 200 pc. The Lyapunov time in these runs does not show
a clear dependence onδ​R\delta R, and the values are consistent with each other within∼1\sim 1%.
We also experimented with swapping two randomly selected particles.
Such a random swap alters the ordering in the summation over particles and
may therefore lead to different round-off effects in the least significant digits of floating-point arithmetic.
In our test simulations, this reordering did lead to different results,
demonstrating that even perturbations at the level of round-off error can affect the outcome.
However, this is not guaranteed, and identical results may still be obtained if the rounding is unaffected by the reordering.
Therefore, we did not adopt particle reordering as a method to generate the perturbed runs in our main experiments.

To quantify the divergence between the reference and perturbed runs, we computed the phase-space distance for each particle as:δi=|𝒙i,0−𝒙i,1|2+|𝒗i,0−𝒗i,1|2,\delta_{i}=\sqrt{|\bm{x}_{i,0}-\bm{x}_{i,1}|^{2}+|\bm{v}_{i,0}-\bm{v}_{i,1}|^{2}},(4)

where𝒙i,0\bm{x}_{i,0}and𝒗i,0\bm{v}_{i,0}are the position and velocity
vector of theii-th particle in the reference run, and𝒙i,1\bm{x}_{i,1}and𝒗i,1\bm{v}_{i,1}are those in the perturbed run. The position and
velocity are expressed in theNN-body unit.

We conducted three experiments:
In Experiment 1, we explored how simulation parameters influence the
system’s chaotic behaviour. Simulations were performed with different
integration time steps (δ​t\delta t), opening angles (θ\theta), and
softening length (ϵ\epsilon), while keeping the total number of
particles fixed toN=107N=10^{7}.

In Experiment 2, we investigated the effect of the particle number,NN, and the softening length,ϵ\epsilon, on the chaotic behaviour of the system.
Here, we variedNNandϵ\epsilonindependently.
The integration time step and the opening angle were fixed toδ​t=0.61\delta t=0.61Myr andθ=0.4\theta=0.4, respectively.

In Experiment 3, we investigated theNN-dependence as in Experiment 2, but we scaled the softening length,ϵ\epsilon, withNN.
We tested two different scaling relations:ϵ=50​pc×(N/107)−1/3\epsilon=50\,\mathrm{pc}\times(N/10^{7})^{-1/3}andϵ=50​pc×(N/107)−1/2\epsilon=50\,\mathrm{pc}\times(N/10^{7})^{-1/2}.
InNN-body simulations, gravitational softening is used to prevent singularities
in the force at small separations, and to improve numerical stability
by smoothing interactions on small scales. Since each simulation
particle represents a finite mass distribution rather than a true
point mass, the softening length should be scaled withNNto reflect
the changing resolution as the number of particles increases.
In three-dimensional systems, the mean inter-particle spacing scales asN−1/3N^{-1/3}, so the first scaling
maintains a constant ratio between the softening length and the
typical particle separation. The second scaling relation achieves the
same effect in two-dimensional systems (such as a flat stellar disc), where
the mean spacing scales asN−1/2N^{-1/2}.

We confirmed that infinitesimal differences in the initial conditions
can grow to large-scale variations in the galaxy, but not all global
quantities are equally sensitive to these chaotic amplifications. In
particular, the bar formation occurs at essentially the same time

model: the bar formation time is essentially constant even when
initial conditions are sampled from the same DF

formation, there is no well-established empirical or theoretical
relation for the buckling time, which suggests that the timing of
buckling is highly susceptible to chaotic variability.

variations in the global structure of the galaxy,

Adopting a smooth potential to model a galactic background, therefore,
has its limitations in terms of reproducing the effect of
chaos. Orbits are nearly smooth in such potentials and unaffected by
punctuated chaos(Portegies Zwart et al.,2023). These orbits, however,

such as orbital diffusion and radial migration, the finite granularity
of the galactic potential cannot be ignored.

## 5Conclusions

The Galaxy is chaotic. To quantify this, we perform 595 tree-codeNN-body simulations with∼106\sim 10^{6}to4×1074\times 10^{7}equal-mass
particles with Galaxy-like (disc and halo) initial conditions. We
varied the number of particles (NN), the interaction time step (δ​t\delta t),
the softening parameter (ϵ\epsilon), and the tree-code opening angle (θ\theta).
Half the number of simulations have newly generated initial realisations.
The other half are copies in which we introduced an infinitesimal perturbation by
radially displacing a randomly selected particle byδ​R=50\delta R=50pc. We study the exponential growth of this initial perturbation
from10−1010^{-10}to unity.

The growth of the perturbation is steep in the first∼100\sim 100Myr,
due to the effect of punctuated chaos. The
later growth is exponential untilt∼2t\sim 2Gyr, after which it
saturates nearδ=𝒪​(1)\delta=\mathcal{O}(1). We fitted the slope of the
logarithmic phase-space distance between 100 Myr and about 1 Gyr,
and interpret its reciprocal as a Lyapunov timescaletLt_{\rm L}.
Disk and halo populations have roughly the same Lyapunov time,
but there is an offset ofδ\deltafor the dark matter halo by three orders of magnitude due
to the larger number of particles.

We established the dependence of the Lyapunov timescale on the number of particles and
the numerical softening length in tree-code calculations (Eq.10).
The Lyapunov timescale seems insensitive to the integration time step and the tree-code opening angle.

Each galaxy withN=107N=10^{7}particles forms a bar at about the same time (1.8 Gyr). The onset
of bar formation is a global event that seems unaffected by the
galaxy’s chaotic behaviour. By the time of bar formation,
the mean phase-space distance has already grown to the size of the galaxy (δ∼𝒪​(1)\delta\sim\mathcal{O}(1)).
Despite this large phase-space variation, the
global formation time of the bar is not affected.
The bar in each simulation evolves differently after its formation.
All the bars buckle eventually, but this happens in a rather broad time window.
The buckling time ranges from around 2.8 Gyr to 4.2 Gyr.

After the maximum bar magnitude, the dispersion in the
bar strength diminishes. This is a somewhat puzzling effect,
because one would naively expect that chaos would continue to drive
the growth of the dispersion. However, if one dumps a lump at
different locations in a pond, the ripples produced will be quite
different, but once they dampen, the pond settles in a new equilibrium
with the same flat surface. Equivalently, a butterfly may initiate a
storm front, but after the storm has settled, the climate has not
noticeably changed.

By comparing the softened tree-code results presented here and inDi Cintio & Casetti (2019)with the non-softened directNN-body
results byGoodman et al. (1993),Hemsendorf & Merritt (2002),
andPortegies Zwart et al. (2022), we conclude that tree-codes are
unable to recover the correct chaotic behaviour of the system. This
fundamental discrepancy can in part be attributed to the introduction
of (too large) softening, and due to the numerical consequence of
adopting an opening angle to clump together far-away particles. The
softening effectively changes the equations of motion and prevents
the system from behaving as chaotically as a granular system because the
dominant driver of chaos has a scale smaller than the
typically adopted softening length.

Smoothed galactic potentials fail to capture the chaotic behaviour ofNN-body systems due to the granularity of the stellar
distributions. Simulations adopting such a smooth
approach should be handled with caution for timescales longer than
the Lyapunov timescale of that particular galaxy. For the Milky Way,
this timescale is expected to be as short as∼105\sim 10^{5}years.

## Ecological impact of this work

FollowingPortegies Zwart (2020), we are concerned about the
ecological impact of our work on the environment. We therefore like
to report on the energy usage of our calculations.
The calculations were performed on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU equipped node.
The total computation required 350 GPU-hours and 560 CPU-hours, consuming 260 kWh and resulting in 110 kg CO2equivalent emissions.

Both authors communicated at conferences they already attended for
other reasons, and through online meetings usingjitsi.com.
