# A sharp Gaussian harmonic-mean inequality for Neumann eigenvalues of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator

## Abstract

Let $N\geq2$ and let $Ω\subset\R^N$ be a connected Lipschitz domain, possibly unbounded, symmetric with respect to the origin, and such that $0<\gammaN(Ω)<1$. We assume that the Gaussian Sobolev embedding $H^1(Ω,\gammaN)\hookrightarrow L^2(Ω,\gammaN)$ is compact; a sufficient condition is the existence of a bounded Gaussian Sobolev extension operator from $Ω$ to $\R^N$. Denote by \[ 0=μ_0(Ω)<μ_1(Ω)\leqμ_2(Ω)\leq\cdots \] the Neumann eigenvalues of the positive Ornstein--Uhlenbeck operator $-Δ+x\cdot\nabla$ in $Ω$. We prove the sharp reciprocal-sum inequality \[ \sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{μ_k(Ω)} \geq \frac{N}{μ_1(B_R)}, \] where $B_R$ is the Euclidean ball centred at the origin and satisfying $\gammaN(B_R)=\gammaN(Ω)$. Equality holds if and only if $Ω=B_R$. The proof combines a coupled $N$-dimensional Ritz argument with a Gaussian raywise rearrangement. The angular imbalance is encoded by a symmetric trace-free matrix, whose contribution is controlled by a finite-dimensional convexity inequality.

A partial result was obtained by Xia and Wang, who proved the sharp(N−1)(N-1)-term inequality∑k=1N−11μk​(Ω)≥N−1μ1​(B)\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{N-1}{\mu_{1}(B)}(1.10)

The same sequence of questions has been pursued on spaces of constant curvature. Ashbaugh and Benguria proved the sharp Szegő–Weinberger inequality for the first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue on hyperbolic space and, under a hemisphere-containment assumption, on the sphere[3]. For domains contained in a hemisphere of𝕊N\mathbb{S}^{N}, Benguria, Brandolini and the author established the sharp harmonic-mean inequality for the firstN−1N-1nontrivial eigenvalues of the Neumann Laplace–Beltrami operator[4]. Xia and Wang obtained the analogous(N−1)(N-1)-term result in hyperbolic space and formulated the full space-form conjecture[21]. Very recently, You and Zhang proved that conjecture: ifΩ\Omegais a smooth bounded domain in a simply connected space form of curvature−1-1or11(withΩ\Omegacontained in an open hemisphere in the spherical case), then∑k=1N1μk​(Ω)≥Nμ1​(BΩ),\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{N}{\mu_{1}(B_{\Omega})},(1.11)

whereBΩB_{\Omega}is a geodesic ball of the same volume, and equality characterises geodesic balls[22]. Thus the coupled matrix method now closes the Euclidean, spherical and hyperbolic reciprocal-sum problems.

Complementary results for the first positive Neumann eigenvalue show that the geometry of the spherical problem is subtler outside the hemisphere regime. Langford and Laugesen proved that a geodesic cap maximisesμ1\mu_{1}among simply connected domains occupying up to94%94\%of𝕊2\mathbb{S}^{2}, thereby extending cap optimality far beyond the hemisphere[16]. On the other hand, Bucur, Laugesen, Martinet and Nahon constructed open subsets of𝕊2\mathbb{S}^{2}of sufficiently large area whose first positive Neumann eigenvalue is strictly larger than that of the equimeasurable geodesic cap[8]. Thus cap optimality may persist beyond a hemisphere under a topological restriction, whereas unrestricted spherical Szegő–Weinberger statements require genuine geometric or topological hypotheses. These results concernμ1\mu_{1}, rather than the reciprocal sum in (1.11), but they clarify the role of the assumptions in the spherical theory.

The Gaussian setting is closely related to spherical geometry, but its Neumann optimisation problem displays a strikingly different behaviour. The standard Gaussian measure can be obtained as the Poincaré limit of normalised surface measures on high-dimensional spheres. Under the same limiting procedure, spherical caps converge to half-spaces, which are the isoperimetric sets for Gaussian measure. Half-spaces also minimise the first Gaussian Dirichlet eigenvalue. It is therefore tempting to expect the Gaussian isoperimetric set to maximise the first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue, as happens in the classical Euclidean and spherical Szegő–Weinberger problems. As already stressed by the author and Di Blasio in[9], this expectation is false.

Indeed, in one dimension, the author and Di Blasio in[9], and Brock, the author and Di Blasio in[7], proved by different methods that, among intervals of prescribed Gaussian measure, the first nontrivial Neumann eigenvalue is minimised by a half-line and maximised by the interval centred at the origin; moreover, the eigenvalue varies strictly monotonically as the interval moves between these two configurations. In higher dimensions, the author and Di Blasio showed that, among origin-symmetric domains of prescribed Gaussian measure, the Euclidean ball centred at the origin uniquely maximisesμ1\mu_{1}[9, Theorem 4.1]. The symmetry restriction does not conceal a possible optimality of half-spaces in the unrestricted problem: every Gaussian half-space has first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue equal to11, independently of its measure, whereas a suitable smoothing of an explicitly chosen non-symmetric square has first eigenvalue strictly larger than11[9, Remark 4.3]. Thus, even after origin symmetry is removed, half-spaces do not maximiseμ1\mu_{1}. On the complementary lower-bound side, Brandolini, the author, Krejčiřík and Trombetti proved in the planar case, under containment in a strip, that equality inμ1​(Ω)≥1\mu_{1}(\Omega)\geq 1characterises strips, and also obtained thin-domain convergence results for eigenvalues and eigenfunctions[6]. Beck and Jerison later removed the strip-containment and dimensional restrictions: for every convex domainK⊂ℝNK\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}, equality holds if and only if, up to rotation,K=ℝ×K′K=\mathbb{R}\times K^{\prime}for a convex domainK′⊂ℝN−1K^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N-1}; their optimal-transport proof does not contain the thin-domain eigenfunction convergence analysis of the former work[5, Theorem I.7].

The lower-order Gaussian problem was advanced by Gao and Wang. In[11], they proved that ifΩ\Omegais an origin-symmetric Lipschitz domain andBRB_{R}is the centred ball satisfyingγN​(BR)=γN​(Ω)\gamma_{N}(B_{R})=\gamma_{N}(\Omega), then∑k=1N−11μk​(Ω)≥N−1μ1​(BR),\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}(\Omega)}\geq\frac{N-1}{\mu_{1}(B_{R})},(1.12)

with equality only forBRB_{R}. Their theorem is the Gaussian counterpart of the(N−1)(N-1)-term estimates of Xia and Wang and of Benguria, Brandolini and the author.

The purpose of the present paper is to complete the Gaussian picture by proving the fullNN-term inequality. Although the finite-dimensional mechanism is inspired by He, Li and Tang and is closely related to the subsequent space-form argument of You and Zhang, the Gaussian problem is not a formal consequence of either result. The operator contains a drift term, the measure is not invariant under translations, the radial volume variable has finite total mass, and a translated Weinberger centre would destroy the radial structure of the weight. Origin symmetry is therefore a genuine structural hypothesis: it makes allNNtransplanted coordinate functions simultaneously orthogonal to the constants. Once this admissibility issue is resolved, the weighted raywise rearrangement produces the same trace-free angular defect that appears in the Euclidean and space-form proofs.

Our main result is the following.

## Appendix ARectangular boxes and the role of centring

The result in this appendix is not needed in the proof of Theorem1.1, but it clarifies the meaning of the symmetry assumption. The Gaussian measure has a product structure, and rectangular boxes form a non-symmetric class in which the first nonzero Neumann eigenvalue can be computed by separation of variables. The one-dimensional theorems in[9,7]show that, at fixed Gaussian measure, every displacement of a factor away from the origin is spectrally unfavourable. Tensorization also forces the Gaussian masses of the factors to be balanced. As a consequence, the centred cube is the unique optimal box.

This observation should be interpreted with some care. It does not prove that the centred ball fails to maximiseμ1\mu_{1}among all non-symmetric domains for the standard Gaussian density; that unrestricted problem remains open. It does show, however, that centring at the origin is an active part of the spectral optimisation rather than a harmless normalisation. It corroborates the need for a centring or symmetry hypothesis in general radial-weight Szegő–Weinberger type results. The argument below uses the product structure of Gaussian measure rather than the radial matrix comparison employed in the main theorem.
