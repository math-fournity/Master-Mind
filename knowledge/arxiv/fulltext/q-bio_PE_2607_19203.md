## Abstract

The Greenland shark (\emph{Somniosus microcephalus}) is among the longest-lived vertebrates known, with female maturity estimated at approximately 156 years and maximum ages approaching four centuries. We apply the theory of evolutionary entropy for age-structured populations to investigate which reproductive windows are compatible with such an extreme demographic profile. Using the homogeneity and critical-threshold theorems for open-group populations, we derive demographic scenarios for generation time, reproductive quantiles, and entropy-maximizing reproductive endpoints. The critical threshold occurs at $q_c\approx0.9763$, in a near-unity persistence regime. Longevity-calibrated scenarios span the boundary separating finite-maximum and asymptotic entropy regimes. These scenarios lead to generation times approaching or exceeding two centuries and reproductive quantiles extending through the third, fourth, and fifth centuries of life, encompassing the age range reported for the oldest Greenland sharks. The critical-threshold equation admits a variational interpretation: its unique solution is precisely the value of $q$ at which evolutionary entropy is maximized. The entropic decomposition $\logλ=H+Φ$ further places these results within the entropic theory of life histories and yields a simple upper bound on the Perron growth rate compatible with the calibrated reproductive persistence. These methods may be applied to obtain quantitative constraints on reproductive windows in species with largely unknown demographic data.

## 1.Introduction

The Greenland shark,Somniosus microcephalus, is among the
longest-lived vertebrates currently known.
Radiocarbon dating of
eye-lens nuclei suggests ages exceeding three centuries, with the
oldest individual estimated at392±120392\pm 120years, while
female sexual maturity has been estimated at156±22156\pm 22years[13].
These observations raise a natural demographic question: what reproductive window is compatible with such an extreme delay in maturity?

We address this question within the entropic theory of life histories
introduced by Demetrius[5,6].
Classical life-history theory, as developed, for example, by Stearns[17]and Charnov[4], describes life
histories through demographic traits and trade-offs involving age at
maturity, survivorship, fecundity, and lifespan. The entropic
formulation complements these descriptors by decomposing demographic
growth into two components. In discrete time, the Malthusian growth
rate isr=log⁡λr=\log\lambda, and(1)log⁡λ=H+Φ,\log\lambda=H+\Phi,

whereHHis evolutionary entropy andΦ\Phiis reproductive
potential. Evolutionary entropy describes the temporal organization
of reproductive contributions through the life cycle, whileΦ\Phiis the complementary component of asymptotic population growth.

The analysis relies on two results established in a recent paper[2]. The homogeneity Theorem 2.4 states
that evolutionary entropy, generation time and reproductive quantiles
depend only on the normalized reproductive distribution. In the same
paper it is shown that there exist two qualitatively different regimes
for evolutionary entropy in the reproductive window: either the entropy
always increases to a horizontal asymptote, or it has a unique interior
maximum.

The critical-threshold Theorem 4.14 states that these regimes, in
open-group Leslie models, are separated by the unique solution of the
criticality equation(2)qA+q−1=0,q^{A}+q-1=0,

whereAAis the age at first reproduction andq=ρ/λq=\rho/\lambda,
withρ\rhodenoting the asymptotic survival parameter of the open
class. In Section4we show that, within the
geometric open-group family, equation (2) is exactly
the stationarity conditiond​H/d​q=0dH/dq=0. By Theorem 4.10 of[2], this stationary point is the unique
maximizer ofH​(q)H(q). The critical threshold therefore has a direct
variational interpretation.

In this paper we consider open-group populations with reproductive persistence
close to the critical threshold, including both subcritical and supercritical
scenarios. For each scenario we compute the generation time, reproductive quantiles
and, when it exists, the entropy-maximizing reproductive endpoint.
We also derive the asymptotic divergence of the optimal reproductive
window as the persistence parameter approaches the critical threshold
from above.

For comparative context, in Section5we apply the same construction to
five other exceptionally long-lived animal species, showing that the
Greenland shark occupies a distinguished demographic position even
within this extreme-longevity group. Our goal is not to reconstruct a demographic matrix forS. microcephalus, but to determine which reproductive windows
are compatible with a maturity age of 156 years.

## 6.Conclusion

The Greenland shark provides an extreme application of the critical-threshold
theory developed in[2]. With female maturity
estimated at approximately156156years, the corresponding critical
thresholdqc≈0.9763q_{c}\approx 0.9763lies remarkably close to 1. The
longevity-calibrated scenarios considered here span this critical boundary,
linking the reported longevity range to both asymptotic and finite-window
entropy regimes.

These results also reveal a different form of sensitivity. Because the age
at first reproduction is exceptionally large, the longevity-calibrated
scenarios considered here lie close to, and straddle, the demographic
critical boundary. Small changes in the effective reproductive ratioq=ρ/λq=\rho/\lambdamay therefore move the model between asymptotic and
finite-window entropy regimes and substantially alter the structure of the
entropy-selected reproductive window. The corresponding reproductive
quantiles span the reported longevity interval of the Greenland shark,
while the finite entropy-maximizing endpoint changes sharply and disappears
altogether on the subcritical side.

sensitivity. The normalized amplification factorΓ​(156)=41.19\Gamma(156)=41.19shows that small changes in reproductive persistence

An interesting possible application of our methods should be emphasized. The critical-threshold theory of[2]converts a single empirically accessible quantity, the age at first reproduction, into quantitative constraints on the entire reproductive window. Even whenλ\lambdaandΦ\Phiare not independently known, evolutionary

temporal organization of reproduction. This may open a route to demographic inference for species where direct reconstruction is out of reach and detailed demographic data are absent.
