## Abstract

Asteroseismology is the study of resonant oscillations of stars to infer their internal structure and dynamics. It is also a powerful tool for precisely determining stellar parameters such as mass, radius, surface gravity, and age. The ongoing TESS mission, with its nearly complete sky coverage, presents a unique opportunity to uniformly probe stellar populations across the Milky Way. TESS is estimated to have observed more than 300,000 oscillating red giants, most of which have one to two months of observations. Given the scale of this dataset, we need a fast, efficient, and robust way to analyse the data. In this work, our objective is to develop a machine learning (ML) based method to infer asteroseismic parameters from short-duration observations. Specifically, we focus on two global seismic parameters, the large frequency separation ($Δν$) and the frequency at maximum power ($ν_{\mathrm{max}}$), from one-month-long TESS observations of red giants. Meanwhile, for K2 data, our focus extends to inferring the period spacings of dipolar gravity modes ($ΔΠ_{1}$), in addition to $Δν$ and $ν_{\mathrm{max}}$. Our findings demonstrate that our machine learning algorithm can accurately infer $Δν$ and $ν_{\mathrm{max}}$ for approximately 50% of samples created by taking one-month Kepler and K2 observations. For TESS one sector data however, we recover reliable $Δν$ for only about 23% of the stars. Additionally, we get reliable $ΔΠ_{1}$ inferences for about 200 young red-giants from K2. For these $ΔΠ_{1}$ inferences, we see a good match with the well known $Δν-ΔΠ_{1}$ degenerate sequence observed in Kepler red-giants.

## IIntroduction

Space-based missions such as CoRoT(Baglinet al.,2006), Kepler(Boruckiet al.,2010), K2(Howellet al.,2014)and TESS(Rickeret al.,2015)have provided vast datasets of high-precision photometric observations. These datasets are expected to increase significantly in size with upcoming missions such as PLATO(Raueret al.,2024).
Precise determinations of fundamental stellar parameters such as mass, radius and age are made possible using asteroseismology. Red-giant asteroseismology is a powerful tool with which to probe populations throughout the Milky Way, enabling ensemble-scale galactic archaeology(Anderset al.,2017; Silva Aguirreet al.,2018; Sharmaet al.,2019; Miglioet al.,2021).
Red giants exhibit mixed modes, which behave as pressure modes in the envelop and gravity mode in the core. These mixed modes probe the stellar core and can place strong constraints on stellar evolution(Beddinget al.,2011; Mosseret al.,2011). These modes also allow us to infer period spacings of gravity modes, which are directly related to core mass(Montalbánet al.,2013).
Kepler has observed∼\sim21,000 oscillating red giants(Honet al.,2019; Dhanpalet al.,2022)in a 100 square-degree field in the northern hemisphere having four years of continuous observations. K2 observed∼\sim19,500 oscillating red giants(Zinnet al.,2022)in 18 Kepler sized fields along the ecliptic, each with three months of observation. TESS, with its vast coverage, is estimated to have observed more than 300,000 oscillating red giants(Mackerethet al.,2021)across the whole sky, with most of them observed for a duration of one to two months. The full sky coverage and an order of magnitude more oscillating red-giants provided by TESS makes it possible to study populations almost uniformly throughout the Milky Way.
Solar-like oscillations have been detected in subgiants and red giants observed by TESS using 1-2 sectors of short-cadence data.Hattet al.(2023)analyzed both 2-minute and 20-second cadence data (Sectors 1 to 46) to identify 4,177 solar-like oscillators, reporting estimates ofνmax\nu_{\text{max}}andΔ​ν\Delta\nu. More recently,Zhouet al.(2024)used 2-minute cadence data (Sectors 1 to 60) and expanded the number of detections to 8,651 stars, also providing estimates of stellar parameters such as radii, masses and surface gravity, in addition to global seismic parameters.
For long-cadence (∼\sim30-minute) data,Silva Aguirreet al.(2020)used 1–2 sectors of TESS observations for bright red giants and detected oscillations in all stars in their sample. Combining seismic inferences with Gaia DR2 parallaxes(Gaia Collaborationet al.,2018), they estimated stellar masses, radii, and ages with typical uncertainties of∼\sim3%, 5%, and 20%, respectively. While these precision levels are comparable to those reported for full-length Kepler observations, it’s worth noting that the dominant source of uncertainty differs: TESS results are largely limited by statistical measurement errors due to shorter time series, whereas for Kepler, systematic uncertainties (e.g., in stellar models and scaling relations) dominate.

Honet al.(2022)analyzed bright HD catalog red giants with 1–2 years of TESS observations near the continuous viewing zones, achieving mass and radius precisions of∼\sim8% and∼\sim3%. They also compared asteroseismic radii from scaling relations with interferometric measurements for 16 stars, finding a median fractional difference of only∼\sim3%.
Furthermore,Mackerethet al.(2021)used up to 13 sectors of data near the TESS continuous viewing zone in the southern hemisphere and recovered a median uncertainty of∼\sim8% in mass and∼\sim26% in age for a subset of the sample when using Gaia-derived luminosities as additional constraints, in combination with asteroseismic inferences. They also show that these ages are precise enough to identify trends in galactic populations.Honet al.(2021)used one sector TESS data and usedνmax\nu_{\text{max}}inferences in combination with parallaxes, radial velocities and proper motions from Gaia EDR3(Gaia Collaborationet al.,2021)to obtain a near all-sky Gaia-asteroseismology mass map, showing potential for all-sky galactic archaeology with TESS.Stelloet al.(2022)analysed Kepler stars observed by TESS in one to two sectors and recoveredνmax\nu_{\text{max}}andΔ​ν\Delta\nuwith uncertainties of∼\sim5% and∼\sim3% respectively. The short duration makes it challenging to infer detailed seismic parameters such asΔ​ν\Delta\nuand they were able to reliably recoverΔ​ν\Delta\nuin only about 20% (14% for one sector and 26% for two sectors) of the population.
Given the vast amount of data from the ongoing TESS mission — with even larger volumes expected from the upcoming PLATO mission — there is a pressing need for fast, efficient, and robust methods to infer seismic parameters from photometric observations.Dhanpalet al.(2022,2023)have developed one such machine-learning–based algorithm, which they applied to Kepler red giants to infer a range of asteroseismic parameters, includingνmax\nu_{\rm{max}},Δ​ν\Delta\nu,Δ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}, andqq, using the full four-year Kepler data sets.
In this work, our goal is to explore the feasibility of inferring these parameters from significantly shorter time series using similar machine-learning techniques. Specifically, we focus on (i) inferringΔ​ν\Delta\nuandνmax\nu_{\rm{max}}from single-sector (∼\sim27 days) TESS observations, and (ii) inferringΔ​ν\Delta\nu,νmax\nu_{\rm{max}}, andΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}from approximately three-month K2 observations, where the longer baseline provides sufficient resolution to recover mixed-mode spacings. Previous work byDavies and Miglio (2016)has already demonstrated thatΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}can be inferred from up to 70 days of data (comparable to K2 campaigns or multiple TESS sectors), but not from 27-day single-sector light curves, based on analyses of varying time series lengths for a young Kepler red giant.


## IIData

## II.1TESS data

We use light-curves detrended using the co-trending basis vector (CBV) correction method implemented in the TASOC (TESS Asteroseismic Science Operations Center) pipeline developed by the coordinated activity TESS Data for Asteroseismology (T’DA) within the TESS Asteroseismic Science Consortium (TASC)(Handberget al.,2021; Lundet al.,2021). We cross-matched the red giants identified byHonet al.(2021)and used light-curves recorded at a cadence of 30 minutes for all the available stars. Instead of raw QLP data, which was used byHonet al.(2021), we use the detrended data from TASOC pipeline because it is optimized for asteroseismology and preserves the stellar oscillation signal better that QLP, which is optimized for planet searches and removes stellar variability over long timescales while performing corrections. These detrended lightcurves from TASOC pipeline are useful for inferringΔ​ν\Delta\nufrom one-month TESS observations. We perform sigma clipping to reject flux values with more than 5-σ\sigmadiscrepancy. Following a similar procedure toStelloet al.(2015), we fill all gaps smaller than 1.5 hours in length using linear interpolation to minimize the impact of windowingGarcíaet al.(2014). Finally, we reject all the stars with greater than 30% empty timestamps at this stage.
We use the lightkurve package(Lightkurve Collaborationet al.,2018)to download light curves hosted at Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST) and compute Lomb-Scargle periodograms(Lomb,1976; Scargle,1982; Press and Rybicki,1989). To make a uniform size sample, we use the same frequency range to calculate the power spectral density (PSD) for all the stars, which is to be fed into the neural network for analysis.
In total, we have 30,720 TESS red-giants for seismic analysis.

## II.2K2 data

We use detrended lightcurves form theK2SFFpipeline(Vanderburg and Johnson,2014)which performs corrections for trends related to spacecraft pointing and other systematics. We take the long cadence (∼\sim30 minutes) data for red-giant stars listed in K2 GAP DR3(Zinnet al.,2022)and high-pass filter each lightcurve with a boxcar filter of width 4 days. Next, we perform sigma clipping to reject flux values with more than 5-σ\sigmadiscrepancy. Finally, we fill gaps shorter than 1.5 hours using linear interpolation similar to TESS data (II.1).
Again, we use the lightkurve package to download light-curves from MAST and compute Lomb-Scargle periodograms for all these stars with the same frequency range.
In total, we have 18,560 K2 red-giants withΔ​ν\Delta\nuin the range1−19​μ​Hz1-19~\mu\rm Hz, out of which 2,176 lie withinΔ​ν\Delta\nurange of9−19​μ​Hz9-19~\mu\rm Hz.

## II.3Training sets

## II.3.1For TESS Model

We combine red giant datasets from the Kepler and K2 missions observations to train a model designed for application to one-month TESS observations; we hereafter refer to this as theTESS-model. For K2, we use data from K2 GAP DR3(Zinnet al.,2022), which provided asteroseismic measurements ofΔ​ν\Delta\nuandνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}}for 19,417 red giants. For Kepler, we use observations of 21,144 red giants, identified by(Dhanpalet al.,2022), who have also provided measurements ofΔ​ν\Delta\nuandνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}}for these stars.
To create training samples comparable in resolution to TESS 1-sector data, we divided each 4-year (in case of Kepler) and 3-month (in case of K2) light-curve into multiple, non-overlapping, 27-day segments. Due to the stochastic excitation of oscillations, each of these shorter-duration lightcurves has a unique noise profile. This stochastic behaviour may also lead to some of these shorter light-curves not showing solar-like oscillations.
Again, we follow the same procedure as described inII.1, filling gaps smaller than 1.5 hrs in duration using linear interpolation and discarding samples having more than 30% empty data points at the end.
Following this, we have 822,274 samples in total, 768,267 from Kepler and 53,980 from K2. We use 70% of these data for training, 15% for validation and the remaining 15% as a test set.
The decision to use observations rather than synthetic data for training is motivated by the well-established links between granulation timescales, oscillation amplitudes, and the location of oscillation power within the PSD(Kallinger and Matthews,2010; Huberet al.,2011; Kjeldsen and Bedding,2011; Mathuret al.,2011; Chaplinet al.,2011; Yuet al.,2018). This was also emphasized byHonet al.(2018), who leveraged binned 2D images of the PSD to detect oscillations and estimateνmax\nu_{\text{max}}. Incorporating these empirical relationships can potentially help the neural network better identify oscillation signatures, especially in short-duration datasets like TESS, where poor frequency resolution and low signal-to-noise ratios pose additional challenges.
We also experimented with training a model on synthetic data, but it significantly underperformed compared to the model trained on observations when evaluated on the Kepler-as-TESS test set. This highlights key differences between synthetic and real data, particularly at low resolution and SNR.
However, training on observations carries the risk of the model internalizingΔ​ν\Delta\nu–νmax\nu_{\text{max}},τconv\tau_{\mathrm{conv}}–νmax\nu_{\text{max}}, and amplitude-scaling relations that may not hold perfectly in the TESS sample. However, as shown byStelloet al.(2022), the overall offsets between Kepler and TESS measurements ofνmax\nu_{\max}andΔ​ν\Delta\nuare small when averaged over the population. Nevertheless, there are mild evolutionary-state–dependent trends, with TESSνmax\nu_{\max}values being typically 2–3% lower for red clump stars nearνmax∼40,μ\nu_{\max}\sim 40,\muHz, and indications of a small positive bias at highνmax\nu_{\max}for red-giant–branch stars. However, the training set remains broadly representative for our purposes, apart from instrument-specific effects such as Earthshine and other systematics.

## II.3.2For K2 Model

Unlike theTESS Model, theK2 Models—which are intended for use with three-month K2 observations (see SectionIII.4)—are trained entirely on synthetic datasets. This approach is necessary due to the scarcity of observedΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}measurements for red giants, which limits the feasibility of training directly on Kepler or TESS data. The synthetic spectra are generated using the simulator developed by Othman Benomar(Benomar,2023), based on the asymptotic theory of stellar oscillations(Aertset al.,2010; García and Ballot,2019). The theoretical framework and detailed methodology for generating these synthetic datasets are described in AppendixA.
We construct two separate datasets to train two distinct models (see SectionIII.4for details), each comprising approximately 10 million synthetic red giant samples. Of these, 82.5% are used for training, 15% for validation, and 2.5% (approximately 320,000 samples) for testing. Both datasets span the same range of parameters, except for differences inΔ​ν\Delta\nuandΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}. The first dataset covers aΔ​ν\Delta\nurange of11–19​μ​Hz19~\mu\mathrm{Hz}andΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}from4040to500500seconds. The second dataset is restricted toΔ​ν\Delta\nubetween99–19​μ​Hz19~\mu\mathrm{Hz}andΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}between4040–150150seconds.
To generate these datasets, we uniformly sample the respective parameter ranges, except for the inclination angleι\iota, which is drawn from an isotropic distribution:P​(ι)∝sin⁡ιP(\iota)\propto\sin\iota. We treatΔ​ν\Delta\nu,Δ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}, andqqas independent parameters. While this approach may result in combinations not strictly supported by stellar evolution theory, it ensures that the training process remains unbiased. ## IIIMethods

We train deep neural networks to infer seismic parameters directly from PSD profiles—using one-month TESS observations for theTESS Model, and three-month K2 observations forK2 Model-1andModel-2, collectively referred to as theK2 Models(see SectionIII.4for details). Inputs to all the models are 1-D arrays of normalized power from the PSDs, without any background correction or feature extraction, and outputs are probability distributions for each parameter (see sectionIII.1). All the relevant features are learned by the neural networks on their own from the full PSD.
Although theTESS ModelandK2 Modelsdiffer in terms of kernel sizes and number of output parameters, the overall architecture is the same for all the models (see sectionIII.2).

## III.1Converting regression problem to classification

Identifying parameters corresponding to the PSD of a given star constitutes a regression problem. However, in our approach, we divided the continuous range of each parameter into a fixed number of bins and trained the model to classify the correct bin number based on the given PSD, effectively recasting the regression task as a classification problem. The model then outputs an array of probabilities, representing the likelihood that each parameter falls into a specific bin.
There are several scientific and practical reasons for this choice. First, classification can improve predictive accuracy, particularly when the relationship between inputs and outputs is highly non-linear(Stewartet al.,2022)or when the output parameters span many orders of magnitude (as is often the case in asteroseismology). Second, classification inherently respects physical constraints — for example, it avoids predicting unphysical negative values for quantities likeΔ​ν\Delta\nuorΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}, which sometimes arise in unconstrained regression models. Lastly, but most importantly, formulating the inference as a classification problem allows us to recover a probabilistic distribution over the parameter space, giving us access not just to a point prediction but to a meaningful measure of the uncertainty and possible multimodality in the inferred values.
As established byRichard and Lippmann (1991), neural network classifiers trained with one-hot encoded target vectors and a cross-entropy loss function yield outputs that estimate Bayesian posterior probabilities. In this context, the output probability distribution over the bins can be interpreted as a quantized approximation to the posterior distribution of the parameter, conditioned on the observed data and the learned model. To validate this capability of the ML models we have shown a comparison of output probabilities from these models with the posteriors obtained using MCMC for a Kepler red giant at K2 resolution (∼88\sim 88days observations) in sectionV.1.
While ML output represents a Bayesian posterior, it is not the same posterior that can be obtained using methods such as Markov-Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). The uncertainties derived from the present distribution are systematically limited due to the finite sizes of the bins, number of training samples used and network complexity. Consequently, MCMC fits could generally provide more accurate values and more precise uncertainties for these stars.
Nonetheless, this method effectively provides an empirical distribution over the parameter range, from which we can extract not only the most probable value (the mode) but also meaningful uncertainty estimates (which are reasonably well calibrated– as reflected by the empirical 1σ\sigmafractions close to the ideal value of 0.683 in subsequent sections). This offers a significant advantage over standard regression based neural networks, which typically do not provide any probability distribution directly. Although techniques like k-fold cross-validation can be employed to estimate uncertainties, this approach requires training multiple instances of the model, substantially increasing training time. Furthermore, this approach often fails to fully capture complex multi-modal behavior, which presents a significant limitation in our specific application because the asteroseismic parameters, especiallyΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}, can exhibit multimodal distributions. Alternatively, mixture density networks(Bishop,1994; Honet al.,2020)can be used to obtain probability distributions, but these necessitate a predefined number of modes and are susceptible to mode collapse. ## III.2Model architecture

In our initial tests, we explored a range of neural network architectures, including a vanilla CNN (Convolutional Neural Network), CNN-LSTM (combining CNNs with Long Short-Term Memory layers), ResNet (Residual Networks), and Vision Transformers (ViT). These architectures were chosen to represent both traditional convolution-based models and more recent attention-based models, allowing us to assess their relative strengths for the task of inferring seismic parameters from photometric power spectra.
Among these, ResNet, which augments CNNs with skip (residual) connections between layers, consistently outperformed the other architectures in both validation loss and stability. We attribute this to ResNet’s ability to mitigate vanishing gradient problems and efficiently capture both local and global patterns in the PSD, which proved particularly useful given the multi-scale features present in the seismic signals. Based on this performance, we selected ResNet as the backbone architecture for both theTESS Modeland theK2 Models, and further optimized it through hyperparameter tuning (see next section).

## III.3Fine-tuning model hyperparameters

To further optimize model performance, we tuned key hyperparameters such as kernel size, learning rate, number of ResNet blocks, and dropout rate.
Based on the results from a grid search, for theTESS Model, we used a smaller kernel size of 21 (compared to 51 for theK2 Models) which account for the lower frequency resolution in the one-month TESS datasets. This choice allows the model to focus on narrower spectral features without oversmoothing. We also found that a higher dropout rate of 0.4 was necessary for theTESS Model(compared to 0.1 for theK2 Models) to mitigate overfitting, due to significant limitations on training data available for TESS. Additionally, the number of ResNet blocks was tuned to balance model complexity against overfitting risk, leading to the selection of a slightly shallower configuration.
The learning rate and batch size were held constant across models (see SectionIII.4) after confirming that these settings provided stable convergence in all cases. Throughout this tuning process, the final hyperparameter choices were determined by the configuration that yielded the lowest validation loss while maintaining consistent performance across multiple training runs.
These design and tuning decisions were guided by empirical performance on the validation set and repeated testing, ensuring that the final models are robust, well-calibrated, and capable of generalizing effectively to unseen data.

## III.4Training

We feed the normalized power from the PSD as input to the model, along with the corresponding one-hot-encoded vectors (bin numbers) for the target output parameters (Δ​ν\Delta\nuandνmax\nu_{\rm max}for theTESS Model;Δ​ν\Delta\nu,νmax\nu_{\rm max},Δ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}, andqqfor theK2 Models). The models are trained as supervised classifiers with the objective of minimizing the categorical cross-entropy loss across all output parameters.
For optimization, we use the ADAM optimizer, which we selected for its robustness and efficiency in handling sparse gradients and adaptive learning rates. To improve convergence, we apply a cosine decay with restarts schedule on the learning rate, which periodically resets the learning rate to avoid local minima and encourage exploration during training.
Training is conducted on 2 GPUs, with an effective initial learning rate of1.5×10−4/21.5\times 10^{-4}/2(scaled by the number of GPUs) and a batch size of 64 per GPU, balancing memory constraints with stable gradient estimates. To ensure the model achieves optimal convergence while mitigating overfitting, we implement early stopping based on the validation loss. Training is halted if the validation loss fails to improve for five consecutive epochs (patience = 5), allowing the model sufficient opportunity to learn meaningful patterns without overtraining on noise or spurious correlations. Throughout training, the validation loss is continuously monitored, and the model state corresponding to the lowest observed validation loss is saved as a checkpoint. This checkpointed model—representing the best generalization performance on unseen data—is then used for inference on both the test dataset and subsequent application samples.

## III.4.1TESS Model

We trained theTESS Modelusing a one-month Kepler/K2-as-TESS dataset to predictνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}}andΔ​ν\Delta\nu, spanning the ranges 8–283μ​Hz\mu\rm Hzand 1–19μ​Hz\mu\rm Hz, respectively. The bin sizes are set to 2μ​Hz\mu\rm Hzforνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}}and 0.1μ​Hz\mu\rm HzforΔ​ν\Delta\nu. Details on the rationale behind these bin sizes are provided in AppendixB. During training, both parameters are given equal weight in the loss function to ensure the model learns to infer them with comparable accuracy and equal attention.

## III.4.2K2 Models

For K2 data, we trained two separate models, each tailored to different parameter ranges, but with identical architectures. Both models outputνmax\nu_{\text{max}},Δ​ν\Delta\nu,Δ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}, andqq, differing only in the parameter ranges they cover:
(i)K2 Model-1operates over aΔ​ν\Delta\nurange of1−19​μ​Hz1-19~\mu\rm Hzand aνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}}range of8−283​μ​Hz8-283~\mu\rm Hz.
(ii)K2 Model-2operates over aΔ​ν\Delta\nurange of9−19​μ​Hz9-19~\mu\rm Hzand aνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}}range of88−283​μ​Hz88-283~\mu\rm Hz.

K2 Model-1is primarily optimized to inferνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}}andΔ​ν\Delta\nu, each assigned a weight of 0.4. We also includeΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}andqqwith smaller weights of 0.1 each. Our experiments show that including these two parameters improves model performance by reducing outliers and better matching theνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}}andΔ​ν\Delta\nuvalues from K2 GAP DR3. These parameters likely helps the model better distinguish mixed modes and avoid mislabeling.
K2 Model-2is composed of two sub-models: one focused onνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}}andΔ​ν\Delta\nu, with weights (0.4, 0.4, 0.1, 0.1) assigned respectively toνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}},Δ​ν\Delta\nu,Δ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}, andqq; and another focused onΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}andqq, with weights (0.1, 0.1, 0.4, 0.4). For predictions, we use theνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}}andΔ​ν\Delta\nuoutputs from the first sub-model, and theΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}andqqoutputs from the second.

The bin sizes for all theK2 Modelsmodels are set to 1μ​Hz\mu\rm Hzforνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}}, 0.05μ​Hz\mu\rm HzforΔ​ν\Delta\nu, 1.25 seconds forΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}, and 0.025 forqq. The methodology for determining these bin sizes is described in AppendixB.

The need for different models arises from the difficulty of inferringΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}from just three months of observations. WhileDhanpalet al.(2023)demonstrated that all four parameters can be inferred from a single model using 4-year Kepler data, shorter observations pose significant challenges. In particular, measuringΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}for evolved red giants and red-clump stars is complicated by the decreasing spacing between successive radial orders and increased granulation noise at low frequencies. Additionally, as stars evolve, mixed-mode inertia increases, reducing mode detectability(Grosjeanet al.,2014), and mixed mode density decreases with the onset of helium burning(Gehanet al.,2018), further complicating period spacing measurements.
Initially, we attempted to inferΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}across the fullΔ​ν\Delta\nurange of1−19​μ​Hz1-19~\mu\mathrm{Hz}, but useful results were obtained only for stars withΔ​ν>9​μ​Hz\Delta\nu>9~\mu\mathrm{Hz}. This led to training a separate model specifically for the9−19​μ​Hz9-19~\mu\mathrm{Hz}range, which proved more effective in inferringΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}within this domain. Therefore, allΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}inferences presented here are derived exclusively fromK2 Model-2, which targets young red giants.

## IVResults

In this section, we first compare the performance of all models on their respective test sets to evaluate and validate prediction accuracy. Subsequently, we apply the models to the target observational datasets—TESS data for theTESS Modeland K2 data for theK2 Models—and present the results of reliable inferences in the form of tables, intended for prospective users.
For these tests, we report the mode of the output probability distributions as the predicted value for each parameter. We choose the mode because the predicted distributions can be broad and could also exhibit multimodal behaviour, particularly forΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}. In such cases, the mode — as the most probable class — offers a more representative estimate than the mean or median, both of which can lie in low-probability regions or fall between distinct peaks. Moreover, due to the typically peaked nature of the softmax outputs in most cases, the mode and median often coincide.
To quantify uncertainty, we compute the difference between the values at the 16th and 84th percentiles relative to the 50th percentile (median) of the output distribution. The total uncertainty is then defined as the absolute sum of these two differences. This approach provides both a sharp point prediction (via the mode) and a meaningful measure of the spread and asymmetry in the predicted distribution (via the percentiles). Furthermore, to assess the calibration and reliability of the reported uncertainties, we use normalized residual, which is defined as:Normalized residual={y^−yσhigh2+σref2,if​y^<yy^−yσlow2+σref2,,ify^≥y,\text{Normalized residual}=\begin{cases}\frac{\hat{y}-y}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\text{high}}^{2}+\sigma_{\text{ref}}^{2}}},&\text{if }\hat{y}<y\\
\frac{\hat{y}-y}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\text{low}}^{2}+\sigma_{\text{ref}}^{2}}},&,\text{if }\hat{y}\geq y,\end{cases}

wherey^\hat{y}is the predicted value,yyis the reference value,σlow,σhigh\sigma_{\text{low}},\sigma_{\text{high}}represent the lower and upper uncertainties on the prediction andσref\sigma_{\text{ref}}the uncertainty on reference value. This definition assumes that the uncertainties in the predictions and reference values are independent, which is a reasonable approximation here since they are derived from different methods, and the reference uncertainties are typically much smaller, being based on substantially longer observations. For a well calibrated model, the empirical 1σ\sigmafraction – defined as the fraction of stars with absolute normalized residuals less than 1 – should be approximately 0.683, corresponding to the 68.3% confidence interval for normally distributed errors. Values higher than 0.683 indicate that the reported uncertainties are too large (the model is under-confident), while values lower than 0.683 indicate that the uncertainties are underestimated (the model is over-confident).
Since our ML models generate predictions for all processed stars without prior vetting, we apply empirically defined uncertainty thresholds to filter and retain only the most reliable inferences. These thresholds vary across models and are determined based on test set performance, by evaluating how closely the model predictions align with reference values. We analyze how this agreement degrades with increasing uncertainty and select cutoff values—typically between 5% and 20%—that preserve predictive accuracy while minimizing the number of outliers.
For example, in the Kepler/K2-as-TESS test set, we find that using aνmax\nu_{\mathrm{max}}uncertainty threshold of 20% retains approximately 85% of the sample, for which 99.1% of predictions have relative errors below 20%. Reducing the threshold to 15% lowers the yield to 78%, while improving accuracy only marginally to 99.4%. Given this trade-off, we adopt the 20% threshold in this case.
Thus, the selected cuts strike a balance between maximizing the usable sample size and maintaining high confidence in the reported parameters.

## VDiscussion

## V.1MCMC and ML Comparison

To validate the capability of our ML models to approximate Bayesian posterior distributions, we compare the network outputs with posteriors obtained using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) analysis for the Kepler red giant KIC 11912315, truncated to K2-like resolution (∼\sim88 days of observation). We employ the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm(Metropoliset al.,1953; Hastings,1970)to sample the posterior, assuming a likelihood based onχ2\chi^{2}with 2 degrees of freedom, given the observed power spectral density (PSD) and a synthetic modelM​(ν,Θ)M(\nu,\Theta). ## V.2Bin Sizes and Uncertainties

While the probabilistic outputs of our neural networks are interpreted as Bayesian posteriors followingRichard and Lippmann (1991), we note an important theoretical nuance regarding the choice of bin sizes explored during our model optimization. Fundamentally, there exists a trade-off in bin sizing between strict theoretical correctness and empirical precision.
For bin convergence exercises (appendixB), we utilized one-hot encoding across all models. While this approach is straightforward for models with larger bin sizes, utilizing one-hot encoding to describe the PSD parameters at the finest bin sizes presents a theoretical challenge. This issue arises because the intrinsic noise in the PSD limits the precision achievable by an ideal theoretical sampler, effectively broadening the posterior. Consequently, the “ideal” theoretical bin size would need to vary dynamically from target to target depending on the local signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). However, fixing a conservatively large bin size globally to accommodate the lowest-SNR targets would be counterproductive, as it would artificially degrade the predictive resolution for the high-SNR targets that are of primary scientific interest.
To assess the extent of this theoretical limitation across our datasets, we note the approximate intrinsic precision limits dictated by the observational baselines. For 3-month K2 data, these theoretical limits are roughly 0.68μ\muHz forνmax\nu_{\rm max}, 0.04μ\muHz forΔ​ν\Delta\nu, 0.1 seconds forΔ​Π1\Delta\Pi_{1}, and 0.01 forqq. For shorter 1-month TESS observations, the intrinsic noise floor is naturally higher, with precision limits of roughly 1.1μ\muHz forνmax\nu_{\rm max}and 0.07μ\muHz forΔ​ν\Delta\nu. When utilizing bin sizes smaller than these thresholds for noise-dominated spectra, representing the target with a single one-hot bin strains the strict mathematical preconditions required for pure Bayesian interpretation.
To address this, we rely on tested machine learning practice and prioritize high-resolution precision. For models trained on synthetic data (e.g., theK2 Models), the true underlying parameters are known exactly. In such cases, while treating the target as a strict one-hotδ\delta-function is a formal theoretical approximation, empirical evidence across astrophysical machine learning demonstrates that prioritizing high-resolution mapping is a proven, effective strategy(e.g., Shuntovet al.,2020; Pasquetet al.,2019; Rotheet al.,2015). Rather than artificially widening the bins, the network implicitly learns the posterior width dynamically from the variance of the training ensemble. During training, the model encounters many similar noisy input spectra corresponding to slightly different exact ground-truth labels. This forces the global minimum to naturally distribute the softmax probability mass across multiple adjacent bins.
The empirical behavior of the network under this approximation is demonstrated in our convergence exercises (AppendixB; Tables3and4), where model performance metrics (e.g., normalized residuals, median uncertainties and 1-sigma fractions) transition smoothly without abrupt changes or qualitative degradation, even as bin sizes are reduced below the aforementioned intrinsic precision limits dictated by the noise. While this empirical robustness does not substitute for formal Bayesian correctness at extreme bin sizes sizes, it underscores the network’s predictive stability when pushed beyond its theoretical constraints.
Crucially, we emphasize that this theoretical limitation primarily affects the extreme lower bounds of our convergence exercises. As shown in AppendixB, the Median Squared Error (MdnSE) for all parameters — in both theK2andTESSmodels — converges at bin sizes that are safelylargerthan these intrinsic precision limits (see Figures12and13, and Tables3and4). As the network achieves optimal performance before reaching the average noise floor, the final asteroseismic parameters reported in this work are derived from models utilizing bin sizes that are larger than the intrinsic precision limits of the PSDs. Consequently, the outputs from our final models are not limited due to this theoretical training constraint and remain robustly interpretable as Bayesian posterior probabilities.
Additionally, for models trained directly on observational data (e.g., theTESS Model), the “ground truth” labels were derived from cataloged central values and implemented as one-hot encoded targets, effectively collapsing the known uncertainties of the reference catalogs into single discrete bins. Treating cataloged central values as exact training targets is a widely used practice in astrophysical machine learning(e.g., Leung and Bovy,2019; Fabbroet al.,2018). Specifically within asteroseismology, deep neural networks have been successfully used in regression tasks for continuous parameters by utilizing central catalog values as exact ground-truth labels(e.g., Honet al.,2018,2021). Thus, while framing this specific task as a discretized classification problem introduces an artificial source of symmetric label noise, deep neural networks are fundamentally mathematically robust to such noise when trained across large ensembles(Rolnicket al.,2017).
Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this discretization inherently acts as an artificial source of label noise. Consequently, the network may struggle to infer posterior widths that are narrower than the intrinsic uncertainties present in the original training catalogs. Our training data contains heterogeneous precision; for instance, the subset fromDhanpalet al.(2022)is inherently resolution-limited to0.1​μ0.1\,\muHz inΔ​ν\Delta\nuand2​μ2\,\muHz inνmax\nu_{\rm max}, whereas the K2 GAP DR3 catalog is not. However, as established during our model validation, the network optimizes its weights using the higher-precision K2 data to generalize beyond the limits of the coarser subsets. When benchmarked against high-precision test sets (Table3), the model achieves median absolute errors of0.04​μ0.04\,\muHz forΔ​ν\Delta\nuand1.5​μ1.5\,\muHz forνmax\nu_{\rm max}. These predictive errors are significantly smaller than the resolution limits of the training subset used byDhanpalet al.(2022), confirming that the model successfully internalizes the highest available precision and is not bottlenecked by heterogeneous label quality.
Finally, in practical terms, this theoretical constraint is negligible for our specific application. Because theTESS Modelis applied to short, one-month observations but was trained on significantly more precise values derived from longer-baseline data, the intrinsic observational noise of the 1-month TESS spectra naturally dominates over the variance floor inherited from the training labels.

## VISummary and Conclusions

We have developed deep learning–based methods to infer key asteroseismic parameters from short-duration photometric observations. ## Appendix BChoosing bin size

To determine the optimal bin sizes for our network architectures, we conducted convergence exercises by training multiple model instances with progressively finer bins. We compared performance across several diagnostics: Median Squared Error (MdnSE), empirical 1σ\sigmafraction, mean/median and standard deviation of normalized residuals, median predicted uncertainty, and the reliable fraction (the number of confident inferences). Together, these metrics capture the effects of binning resolution on model accuracy, precision, calibration, and outlier behavior. We utilized MdnSE rather than Mean Squared Error because it is significantly more robust to extreme outliers, thereby more accurately reflecting the model’s baseline performance across diverse bin configurations.

While strictly varying one parameter at a time across our entire multi-dimensional grid constitutes an ideal controlled experiment. Our convergence exercises primarily utilize a multi-variable sweep to efficiently map the parameter space without training approximately 60 separate models, supplemented by targeted single-parameter control models for most sensitive parameters (discussed below) to demonstrate empirical robustness of this approach.

Finally, as detailed in SectionV.2, we note that utilizing strict one-hot encoding at finest bin sizes stretches the theoretical preconditions for interpreting model outputs when the bin width falls below the intrinsic noise limit of the data. We refer the reader to SectionV.2for a comprehensive discussion on this trade-off between empirical precision and formal mathematical correctness.

## Appendix DPeriod Spacings Selection Cut

No cuts based onqqor any other parameters are applied, ensuring that this analysis remains independent of other model outputs.

We also track the anomalous fraction—the proportion of stars within each selected subset flagged as anomalous predictions based on the criteria defined in SectionIV.2.2. The difference between the outlier and anomalous fractions therefore indicates the number of unidentified outliers that remain within the selected subset.
The optimal threshold should maximize coverage while maintaining high accuracy and a low fraction of unidentified outliers.
