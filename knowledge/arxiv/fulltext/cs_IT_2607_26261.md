## Abstract

Two linear error-correcting codes $\cal{C}_1, \cal{C}_2 \subseteq \mathbb{F}_q^n$ are called linearly equivalent if there is a linear isometry mapping $\cal{C}_1$ to $\cal{C}_2$. In this work, we generalize the notion of linear equivalence and study the minimum distortion $\cal{D}(\cal{C}_1, \cal{C}_2)$ of a linear mapping between codes $\cal{C}_1, \cal{C}_2 \subseteq \mathbb{F}_q^n$, which quantifies how similar $\cal{C}_1$ and $\cal{C}_2$ are. We introduce and study the Code Distortion Problem (CDP), which asks to find a minimum distortion mapping between two input codes $\cal{C}_1$ and $\cal{C}_2$. CDP generalizes the Linear Code Equivalence Problem (LCE), which is essentially the special case of CDP where $\cal{D}(\cal{C}_1, C_2) = 1$ and which is well-studied because of its role in cryptography.   We prove that (decisional) CDP is $\mathsf{NP}$-hard to approximate to within any constant factor, and that it is in $Σ_2^P$. We also give a single-exponential-time $k^2$-approximation algorithm for CDP, where $k$ is the dimension of the input codes. Furthermore, we give a single-exponential-time $\big(\frac{2k + 1}{3})^2$-approximation algorithm for a natural special case of CDP, and we show that our analysis is tight in this case.   We use techniques from analogous work on the Lattice Distortion Problem (LDP) by Bennett, Dadush, and Stephens-Davidowitz (ESA, 2016). We also introduce or study a number of additional concepts that might be of independent interest. These include an adaptation of the celebrated reduction of Goldreich, Micciancio, Safra, and Seifert (IPL, 1999) from the Shortest Vector Problem (SVP) to the Closest Vector Problem (CVP) on lattices to the analogous problems on codes; successive minima bases for codes; and the matrix $0 \to 0$ "norm" on subspaces.

## 1Introduction

Alinear error-correcting code(or simply,code) is a linear subspace𝒞⊆𝔽qn\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}.
Although best known for their use in robust communication, codes also appear prominently in algorithms, computational complexity, and cryptography. Notably, several of the cryptosystems appearing in late rounds or standardized by the National Institute of Standards and Technology’s (NIST) post-quantum cryptography standardization process are based on the intractability of certain computational problems on codes[ABC+22,BMPS20,GAA+25].

One central problem on codes of cryptographic interest is theLinear Code Equivalence Problem(LCE\mathrm{LCE}), in which, given generator matrices of two codes𝒞1,𝒞2⊆𝔽qn\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}as input, the goal is to decide whether𝒞1\mathcal{C}_{1}and𝒞2\mathcal{C}_{2}arelinearly equivalent, i.e., whether one can convert𝒞1\mathcal{C}_{1}into𝒞2\mathcal{C}_{2}simply by permuting coordinates and scaling coordinates by non-zero values.
In particular, the LESS cryptosystem[BMPS20,BBPS21]crucially relies on the hardness ofLCE\mathrm{LCE}. It is currently a second-round candidate in NIST’s “Additional Digital Signature Schemes” standardization process[Nat25].
Furthermore, a large body of work has studiedLCE\mathrm{LCE}and its variants from an algorithmic and complexity-theoretic standpoint. See, e.g.,[Leo82,PR97,Sen00,BCGQ11,Beu20,BBPS23,DG23,BW24,Now25,BBB+26].

A linear isometry (i.e., distance-preserving map) on𝔽qn\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}corresponds to a matrixM∈𝔽qn×nM\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n\times n}such that for all𝒙∈𝔽qn\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},∥M​𝒙∥0=∥𝒙∥0\lVert M\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{0}=\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{0}, where∥⋅∥0\lVert\cdot\rVert_{0}denotes the Hamming weight of a vector.

In other words, the two operations in the definition ofLCE\mathrm{LCE}—permuting and scaling coordinates by non-zero values—exactly characterize the linear isometries on𝔽qn\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}.

## 1.3An Approach for a Faster Approximation Algorithm

A natural direction for making our approximation algorithm inTheorem1.2more efficient would be to use reduced (but not necessarily optimal) bases in place of successive minima bases.
Indeed, this is how[BDS16]achieves a time-approximation tradeoff in its algorithm forLDP\mathrm{LDP}, which includes a polynomial-time approximation algorithm in one regime.
(On the other hand, our approximation algorithm inTheorem1.2only runs in polynomial time for an extreme parameter regime—whenk=O​(logq⁡n)k=O(\log_{q}n).)
And, a recent line of work has studied basis reduction algorithms for codes[DDvW22,GS24], including analogs of basis reduction algorithms for lattices.
However, it is not clear how to leverage bases reduced in any of the senses they consider to get an approximation algorithm running in a reasonable amount of time and with a reasonable approximation factor.

## 1.4Open Questions

Our work leaves open several interesting questions. On the complexity side, our results showing thatGapCDP\mathrm{GapCDP}is both𝖭𝖯\mathsf{NP}-hard and inΣ2𝖯\Sigma_{2}^{\mathsf{P}}begs the question of whetherGapCDP\mathrm{GapCDP}is alsoΣ2𝖯\Sigma_{2}^{\mathsf{P}}-hard, and thusΣ2𝖯\Sigma_{2}^{\mathsf{P}}-complete. We note that a fairly simple reduction fromLCE\mathrm{LCE}to the Lattice Isomorphism Problem (LIP\mathrm{LIP})—problems that essentially correspond to the distortionD=1D=1cases ofCDP\mathrm{CDP}andLDP\mathrm{LDP}, respectively—was given in[Reg14,BW24].

## 2.6Reductions

In this paper, we employ three different types of reductions. The first and most general notion is that of aTuring reduction. Formally, there is a Turing reduction from a decision problemAAto a decision problemBBif and only if there exists an algorithm forAA, given oracle access toBB. In other words,AATuring-reduces toBBif, given a subroutine forBB, one can decideAA. The second type of reduction is aCook reduction. Formally, a Cook reduction is a polynomial-time Turing reduction, i.e.,AACook-reduces toBBif and only ifAATuring-reduces toBBand the overall algorithm runs in polynomial time. The third and final type of reduction is that of aKarp reduction(a.k.a., apolynomial-time many-one reduction). Formally, a Karp reduction is a deterministic and polynomial-time algorithm that maps YES and NO instances ofAAto YES and NO instances ofBB, respectively.
