## Abstract

We investigate traveling fronts in a lossy nonlinear transmission line. By prescribing a hyperbolic-tangent profile with dimensionless width $m$, we solve the inverse problem of determining the voltage--charge relation for which this profile is an exact solution. The exact relation is expressed in terms of the Gauss hypergeometric function. We also construct an approximate solution by assuming a cubic voltage--charge relation and replacing the resulting quadratic damping function with its optimal linear least-squares approximation. This construction determines the cubic coefficients and yields a modified governing equation that admits the prescribed profile as an exact solution. In both constructions, physical admissibility selects a shock front. In the broad-front limit, the approximate normalized force agrees with the exact result through order $m^{-2}$; numerical comparisons show that the agreement remains good even outside the asymptotic region of large $m$. Finally, a characteristic analysis of the original partial differential equations distinguishes compressive shocks from noncompressive kinks without recourse to the mechanical analogy.

## Abstract

We investigate traveling fronts in a lossy nonlinear transmission line. By
prescribing a hyperbolic-tangent profile with dimensionless widthmm, we solve
the inverse problem of determining the voltage–charge relation for which this
profile is an exact solution. The exact relation is expressed in terms of the
Gauss hypergeometric function. We also construct an approximate solution by
assuming a cubic voltage–charge relation and replacing the resulting
quadratic damping function with its optimal linear least-squares
approximation. This construction determines the cubic coefficients and yields
a modified governing equation that admits the prescribed profile as an exact
solution. In both constructions, physical admissibility selects a shock
front. In the broad-front limit, the approximate normalized force agrees with
the exact result through orderm−2m^{-2}; numerical comparisons show that the
agreement remains good even outside the asymptotic region of largemm.
Finally, a characteristic analysis of the original partial differential
equations distinguishes compressive shocks from noncompressive kinks without
recourse to the mechanical analogy.

## IIntroduction

Nonlinear electrical transmission lines provide both useful circuit
elements and experimentally accessible models of nonlinear-wave
propagation. Voltage-dependent capacitance or current-dependent
inductance makes the local wave speed depend on the wave amplitude,
leading to wave steepening, harmonic generation, solitary waves, and
shocks. In
an early analysis, Landauer showed that capacitive nonlinearity can
transform a periodic signal into an electromagnetic shock and emphasized
the role of dissipation in determining the subsequent shock
evolution[1]. Electrical networks were later used as controlled
lattices for observing soliton propagation and
interaction[2]. Soliton-like signals in nonlinear electrical
transmission lines have also been constructed, and their linear
stability has been analyzed within a modified complex Ginzburg–Landau
description[3]. Related multiscale reductions of composite
right- and left-handed nonlinear transmission lines predict coupled
backward- and forward-propagating vector solitons[4].
Experiments on nonlinear left-handed transmission lines have also
demonstrated multistability, period doubling, and the onset of chaotic
dynamics at higher input powers[5].
Stationary and traveling localized modes have also been observed
experimentally and reproduced numerically in driven two-dimensional
nonlinear electrical lattices[6]. More generally, the
existence, stability, and velocity-dependent dynamics of moving
discrete breathers have been investigated in nonlinearβ\beta-Fermi–Pasta–Ulam lattices[7].

These effects also have direct technological applications. Varactor-loaded
nonlinear transmission lines compress electrical wave fronts and generate
very short pulses. Monolithic GaAs implementations produced electrical
steps with fall times below1.4​ps1.4\,\mathrm{ps}and enabled
millimeter-wave sampling[8]. Nonlinear LC transmission lines
with cubic capacitive
nonlinearity have also been proposed as analog-gravity systems, in
which a voltage soliton creates an effective event horizon for
electromagnetic waves[9]. On the mathematical side,
solutions of continuum models with
voltage-dependent capacitance, resistance, and leakage conductance have
been shown to develop shock discontinuities from sufficiently steep
initial data[10]. Thus, nonlinearity supplies the steepening
mechanism, while dissipation regularizes the discontinuity into a
traveling front of finite width.

Most studies begin with a specified circuit voltage–charge relation and
then determine the resulting nonlinear waves. The complementary design
problem—determining a voltage–charge relation that supports a prescribed
front profile—has received less attention. Factorization approaches
provide an analytical route for nonlinear second-order
differential equations with polynomial
nonlinearities[11]. Related exact-solution and integrability
techniques have been applied to the Duffing–Van der Pol
equation[12], while exact traveling-wave solutions of a
nonlinear reaction–diffusion equation were obtained by
Kaliappan[13]. In our previous work, we
derived traveling-wave equations for lossy nonlinear transmission
lines and identified conditions under which traveling-front profiles can
be expressed in elementary form[14,15]. Here we solve the
inverse problem of determining the voltage–charge relation that supports a
prescribed
hyperbolic-tangent
profile. The resulting exact relation is expressed in terms of the
Gauss hypergeometric function.
We also improve the approximate solution presented in our earlier work.

## VIIConclusions

We studied traveling fronts in a lossy nonlinear transmission line. We solved
the inverse problem exactly and obtained the voltage–charge relation that
supports a hyperbolic-tangent shock profile. This exact construction expresses
the voltage–charge relation in terms of the Gauss hypergeometric function and
three front parameters: the total charge jump2​Δ​Q2\Delta Q, the dimensionless
widthmm, and the dimensionless squared wave speedww.

We also obtained an approximate solution by assuming a cubic voltage–charge
relation and replacing the resulting quadratic damping function with its
optimal linear
least-squares approximation.

In the broad-front limitm≫1m\gg 1, the exact
normalized forceF~​(v)\widetilde{F}(v)agrees through orderm−2m^{-2}with the approximate result in Eq. (53).
The numerical comparisons show that the agreement remains good even outside
the asymptotic region of largemm, improves for broader fronts, and
deteriorates asm→1+m\to 1^{+}, the boundary of the physically admissible range.

Finally, an analysis of the long-wavelength characteristics of the original
partial differential equations recovers the kink–shock distinction without
using the mechanical analogy: shocks satisfy a Lax-type compressivity
condition, whereas kinks are noncompressive.
